The Task Test

They say…It’s about…Because…
“Cups face label-out — redo that row”The taskNames the thing and the fix — correction
“Next time, sweep BEFORE you mop”The taskIt’s Week 13’s ordering lesson, free of charge
“Watch the clock on breaks, okay?”The taskA behavior, a standard, a reset — still correction
Name-calling, mocking, yelling at YOU as a personNot okayThat’s not feedback — that’s a problem for a supervisor (next week covers exactly this)

Why your brain mixes them up

Correction arrives fast, sometimes blunt, often mid-rush — and the body reads FAST + BLUNT as attack. The task test is the three-second fix: “Is this about a TASK I can change?” If yes — it’s free coaching wearing a gruff voice. Busy managers correct in seven words; the shortness is the schedule talking, not anger.

Run the Task Test

Mid-rush, the manager says fast and flat: “Wrong bags — green for cold items.” The task test says…
Seven words, one task, one fix — textbook correction in a busy voice. Waiting for corrections to arrive gift-wrapped means missing most of your training.
A coworker rolls their eyes and mutters “of course YOU messed it up again” in front of customers. Task test?
No task, no fix, plus an audience = not feedback. You don’t owe that a professional feedback response — and you don’t match it either. (Week 17: gossip, arguments, and getting a supervisor.)
You did the shelf EXACTLY how the trainer showed you — and the manager corrects it anyway. First move?
Being corrected while “right” is the hardest one — and the calm later-question (hello, Week 15 toolbox) sorts it without a standoff. Two bosses, two methods happens everywhere; you just need the tiebreaker.
Why do the workers who get corrected the MOST in their first month often turn out to be the best workers by month six?
Think first — then click to reveal
Corrections flow toward workers people bother to train — silence is what managers give workers they’ve given up on. Every correction is a deposit: someone spent their time making you better. Month six is when the deposits pay out.

Accept • Confirm • Apply

PartSay / doExample: “Cups face label-out”
1. Accept — five words, no armor“Got it” • “Thanks for catching that” • “My mistake”“Oh — got it, thanks.”
2. Confirm — prove it landedWeek 13’s echo, feedback edition: say the fix back“Label-out on every row?”
3. Apply — visibly, soonFix it now or name when: the response managers actually rememberTurns the cups. Right then.

The response killers

Excuse-first (“well NOBODY told me—”) — even when true, it’s step four, never step one. The armor words — “whatever,” “I KNOW,” “fine.” The silent version — face says everything the mouth didn’t. Over-apologizing — a five-minute sorry spiral makes it weird; one clean “my mistake” and MOVE. The fix is the apology.

Day 2 • You Try It

Sixty-Second Drills

“This mop water needed changing two tables ago.” Best full response?
Accept, confirm, apply — nine words and a bucket. The last-shift fact might even be true; it can come up LATER, calmly, after the fix. Excuse-first erases everything said after it.
You say “got it, thanks” perfectly — while sighing, eyes to the ceiling. What did the manager hear?
Week 14’s rule, sharpest edge: the dials can cancel the words. Under embarrassment, the face dial slips first — which is why we drill the whole delivery, not the sentence.
The correction is about something you can’t fix RIGHT now (mid-task, hands full). Apply looks like…
Apply isn’t always instant — but it’s always SCHEDULED, out loud. The named when plus the follow-through is what separates “heard you” from “handled it.”
“Thanks for catching that” — thanking someone for pointing out YOUR error. Why does that strange little sentence work so well on managers?
Think first — then click to reveal
It flips the moment: instead of two people in an awkward standoff, it’s two people on the same team against the mistake. Managers correct all day and mostly get armor back — the worker who says thanks becomes the one they WANT to train. It’s Day 1’s deposit theory, activated by you.

Own It • Fix It • Prevent It

StepWhat it looks likeExample: you priced a whole shelf wrong
1. Own it — fast, plainlyTell who needs to know, before it’s found: “I made a mistake on ___ — wanted you to know”“I think I tagged the sauces at the old price — wanted to flag it.”
2. Fix it — or help fix itWhat CAN you repair right now? Offer it: “I can retag them now if that works”Retags the shelf — fifteen minutes.
3. Prevent it — fix the leakWhat let it happen? Checklist line, double-check habit, a question you should’ve askedNew habit: check one tag against the sheet before doing the row.

Here’s the math managers know: a mistake owned early is a fifteen-minute story; the SAME mistake discovered later is a trust story — and trust stories are the expensive kind. Nobody was ever fired for “I made a mistake and here’s my fix.” Plenty have been for the hiding. And when the mistake isn’t yours? Same routine minus the owning: flag it, help fix it, no detective work about whose it was.

Handle It

You spot YOUR mistake from an hour ago — wrong bags, cold items, customer long gone. Nothing to physically fix. Own-fix-prevent looks like…
The heads-up IS the fix here — now the manager can handle a call smoothly instead of blindsided. Prevention still applies. Unfixable mistakes still get owned; they just skip step two.
You find a spill in aisle 3. Not yours. Nobody around. You…
A spill is a safety signal (Week 15!) wearing a mistake costume. Handle first; blame never. “Whose was it” fixes exactly nothing.
Same small mistake, third time this month — you keep forgetting to relock the stockroom. The step you’re skipping is…
Repeat mistakes are prevention failures wearing new dates. Sorry number three sounds exactly like sorry number one — the checklist line is what changes the story.
Owning a mistake nobody would ever discover — why do it? Build the honest case, both sides.
Think first — then click to reveal
The case against: awkward, maybe unnecessary. The case for: if it surfaces later you’re protected; your manager gets to trust your reports completely; and you get to be someone whose word covers ALL of it, not just the visible parts. One awkward minute buys a reputation that pays for years — it’s the signature promise from Week 5, kept when no one’s checking.
