What Is a Worker Profile?

A worker profile is…

A picture of you as a future worker: your interests (what you like), your strengths (what you do well), and your needs (what support helps you succeed).

Why it matters

People do their best work at jobs that fit them. A cook who loves quiet might not enjoy a loud kitchen. A talker might love a front counter. Fit comes first.

Warm-up — talk with a partner

  • What jobs do people in your life have?
  • What is something you are good at?
  • What do you like doing so much you lose track of time?

The Big Five — Know Them Cold

Every form asks for…

  1. Full legal name
  2. Address (street, city, state, ZIP)
  3. Phone number
  4. Date of birth
  5. Emergency contact (name + number)

Watch me (I do → we do)

I’ll fill out a sample form on the board and make two common mistakes — watch for them: skipping a box, and writing a nickname where it says legal name. Catch me!

Who gets your information?

Yes: employers, doctors, school, trusted family.  No: strangers, unknown callers, websites you don’t know, anyone who pressures you to hurry.

Day 2 • You Try It

Share It or Protect It?

The form says legal name. You write…
Legal name = the name on your ID and birth certificate. Nicknames confuse payroll, records, and paychecks.
A caller says: “I’m from the bank — hurry, I need your address and birthday RIGHT NOW.” You…
Real banks never rush you for personal info by phone. Pressure to hurry is the #1 scam signal.
Who should get your personal information?
Trusted people and places with a real reason to need it — that’s the whole rule.
Which of the Big Five protects you in an emergency — and why should someone besides you know it?
Think first — then click to reveal
Emergency contact. If you’re hurt, sick, or stranded, it connects helpers to your trusted person fast — even if you can’t speak for yourself.

The Count-Up Strategy

Start big, count up

Sort your money largest first: bills, then quarters, dimes, nickels, pennies. Count up as you go: “10… 15… 16 dollars… 16.25… 16.35.”

Watch me (I do → we do)

I’ll count a handful of practice money out loud on the document camera. Then we count one together as a class — you call out the running total.

Quick reference

Quarter = 25¢  •  Dime = 10¢  •  Nickel = 5¢  •  Penny = 1¢  •  4 quarters = $1

Watch the Count-Up Work

Example B — coins only

Example C — two price tags
