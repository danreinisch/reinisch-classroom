Ask NOW vs. Try First

SignalAsk NOW — no trying first
SafetyAnything that could hurt you or someone — unfamiliar machine, spill, lifting past your limit. Guessing here is the one unforgivable
BrokenEquipment down, register frozen, door won’t lock — you can’t fix what you don’t own
Money & customersRefunds, an upset customer, a register that won’t balance — above a new worker’s pay grade on purpose
Truly lostYou don’t know what the task even IS — no starting point means no try to make

Everything else: the try-first rule

Give it one honest try — check the checklist, look at how it was done last time, apply the two ordering questions. Still stuck after one real attempt? Ask — and now your ask is BETTER: “I tried ___, and I’m stuck at ___.” That sentence tells the manager you’re a worker, not a passenger.

Day 1 • You Try It

Ask or Try?

The box cutter’s blade is loose and wobbling. Ask now or try first?
Safety asks are ALWAYS now. No manager alive says “I wish you’d improvised with the broken blade.”
You’re shelving stock and can’t remember if sauces face label-out or label-up. Ask now or try first?
One honest try: the last worker’s shelf IS the instruction. If the whole aisle is chaos — then ask, and your ask carries proof you looked.
A customer wants a refund and is getting loud. You’ve never done a refund. Ask now or try first?
Getting the manager IS the correct move, not a failure — refunds are gated on purpose. “Let me get someone who can take care of you” is service, not surrender.
The try-first rule says one honest try makes your ask BETTER. What’s the difference between “How do I do this?” and “I tried ___ and I’m stuck at ___” — to the manager’s ears?
Think first — then click to reveal
The first says “do my thinking for me.” The second says “I think — I just hit a wall.” Same help needed, opposite reputations. The try is what turns asking from a cost into a credit.

Five Phrases, Five Kinds of Stuck

Kind of stuckThe phrase
Missed it — too fast, too quiet“Could you say that once more?”
Need to see it — words aren’t enough“Could you show me the first one, and I’ll do the rest?”
Which first — two tasks, one you“Do you want A or B first?”
Almost sure — one detail wobbling“Just to check — I should ___, right?”
Tried and stuck — the wall“I tried ___, and I’m stuck at ___. Could you help?”

Notice what every phrase has in common: each one is SPECIFIC about what you need — a repeat, a demo, an order, a confirm, a rescue. Vague stuck (“I don’t get it”) makes the helper do detective work first. The right phrase skips straight to the help — delivered in last week’s voice, dials set.

Day 2 • You Try It

Pick the Phrase

The trainer demonstrated the label gun once, fast, and your first three price tags came out crooked. Which phrase?
Crooked tags after a fast demo = a see-it problem. The “I’ll do the rest” half matters — it promises you’re borrowing a demo, not the whole task.
You’re 90% sure the mop water gets dumped in the FLOOR drain, not the sink — but 90% isn’t 100%. Which phrase?
The confirm phrase costs five seconds and shows your thinking. Wrong-drain confidence costs a plumber. “Just to check” is the cheapest insurance at any job.
Manager, walking past: “Restock the cooler and face the shelves.” Both look urgent. Which phrase?
Week 13’s question, now in its toolbox home. Either/or asks are the fastest in the box — managers answer them mid-stride without breaking step.
Build the “tried and stuck” phrase for this: you’re assembling a display stand, got the base and poles together, but the top shelf won’t click in after three tries. Say it out loud, dials set.
Think first — then click to reveal
“I got the base and poles together, but the top shelf won’t click in — I’ve tried it three ways. Could you take a look?” Specific try + specific wall + clear ask. That’s a worker’s sentence.

The Line Between

Sounds like refusalWhat a worker says instead
“I can’t do this.” (walks away)“I’m stuck at ___ — could you help me get past it?” — help keeps you IN the task
“That’s not my job.”“I haven’t been trained on that yet — want me to grab someone, or can you show me?”
“I’m not lifting that.”“That’s past my safe lifting limit — can we team-lift it, or is there a cart?” — an honest limit PLUS a path forward
(silently doesn’t do it)Anything out loud — silent skipping reads as refusal even when it was fear

The pattern

Refusal closes the task: no, not me, walks off. A worker’s sentence keeps it open: names the real barrier (stuck, untrained, unsafe) + offers a path (help me, show me, team-lift, get someone). Honest limits are respected at every good job — especially safety limits, which you NEVER apologize for. It’s the closed door that costs you, not the limit.

Day 3 • You Try It

Which Is It?

“I’ve never used the slicer, and honestly it makes me nervous. Could someone train me before I take that station?” Help or refusal?
Naming nerves around a blade is SMART, and “train me before” keeps the door wide open. That’s exactly the sentence safety-minded managers want to hear.
Asked to mop, a coworker says “nah” and keeps scrolling. Same coworker later says they were “just asking for help.” What made it refusal?
Run the pattern: barrier? none. path? none. task? closed. Help-seeking has a shape, and “nah + scroll” isn’t it — no tone dial can save that one.
Your manager asks you to carry a load you know is past your safe limit. The RIGHT response…
An honest limit + a path forward is the strongest sentence on this slide. Hurting yourself to avoid an awkward moment is the worst trade at any job — and good managers know it.
Why does SILENT skipping — just quietly not doing the task — read as refusal, even when the real reason was fear or confusion?
Think first — then click to reveal
The manager can’t see inside your head — they see an undone task and no words. Fear and refusal look identical from the outside; only your voice can tell them apart. That’s why every worker sentence this week happens OUT LOUD.
