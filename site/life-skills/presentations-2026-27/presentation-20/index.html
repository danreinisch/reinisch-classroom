The Pickup and the Return

SituationScriptThen…
You answer“Hello, this is [first name].”Stop. Let them talk. Don’t fill silence with “um” or “who is this”
You missed itListen to the voicemail fully BEFORE calling back — every detail once, not three frantic half-listensCall back within the hour if possible; same business day, always
Return call script“Hi, this is [name] returning a call from [manager/employer name] about [position]. I’m available to talk now, or I can call back at a better time — my number is [repeat slowly].”If you reach voicemail: leave the same info
Take a message“They’re not available right now. May I take a message?” → write: name + number + reasonDeliver it immediately, don’t trust memory

Two rules that separate pros from panickers: listen fully before calling back (one careful listen beats three rushed ones), and repeat your number slowly — the person on the other end is writing it down. The Week 14 voice applies to every line: volume up, speed down, face & body steady even when it’s just your voice they can hear.

Day 1 • You Try It

Pickup and Return

A number you don’t recognize calls while you’re at lunch. Best move?
Job search mode means unknown numbers get the professional pickup. Screening all calls loses opportunities on the first ring — and “yeah?” sets a tone you can’t unset.
You missed a call and the voicemail says “Hi, this is Jordan at Riverside Diner about your application — please call 555-0143.” You listen once and catch “Jordan” and “143.” You…
Missing details mid-call (“wait, who is this?”) is a rough start. Thirty extra seconds of listen-and-write buys a confident first sentence. Same-day return is always the goal.
In your return-call script, you give your number. Which delivery is better?
Caller ID misses the voicemail scenario and the “my phone didn’t capture that” reality. Slow numbers = callbacks. Fast ones = deleted voicemails.
Why should your return-call script mention the POSITION by name, not just “I’m calling about my application”?
Think first — then click to reveal
Employers often hire for multiple positions at once — “the application” leaves them guessing which pile you’re in. “The part-time prep cook position” puts them in the right folder in one second. It also shows you paid attention when you applied — which is already about the job.

The Five-Part Leave

PartSay exactlyExample
1. WhoYour full name, first time slowly“Hi, this is Alex Rivera —”
2. WhyPosition name, where you applied“— I’m calling about the kennel assistant position at Paws & Claws—”
3. WhenBest times to reach you, specific“— I’m available Monday through Friday after 3pm—”
4. NumberTwice: once medium, once slow“— my number is 555-0143, again that’s 5…5…5… 0…1…4…3—”
5. ThanksBrief, genuine close“— thank you for your time, I look forward to hearing from you.”

Total length: under 30 seconds. Over 30 seconds and they stop listening; under 10 and they can’t write your number. The slow-number-twice habit is non-negotiable: imagine the manager scribbling with one hand while pouring coffee with the other. Give them two chances, not one.

Day 2 • You Try It

Build the Leave

Which five-part leave is most likely to get called back?
Option A: complete, 22 seconds, number twice. Option B: zero information. Option C: over 45 seconds, number once at speed — both B and C end in the deleted folder.
You’re only free Saturday mornings. You say “I’m available anytime” in the voicemail. What’s the problem?
Week 4’s availability lesson, wearing a phone headset. Honest and specific availability in a voicemail protects both sides from the frustrating callback dance. “Saturday mornings, or I can call at your convenience” is more useful than “anytime.”
You leave the perfect voicemail. The employer doesn’t call back in 48 hours. You…
One good follow-up voicemail is professional. The rule is roughly: one message, wait 2-3 business days, one follow-up. After that, keep applying elsewhere. Persistence and harassment have a clear line.
Why do you give your number TWICE — once at medium pace, once slowly — rather than just once, carefully?
Think first — then click to reveal
The first delivery helps them hear the number; the second gives them time to write it. A manager catching a voicemail mid-task needs that second pass or they have to replay the whole message — and busy managers often don’t. Two paces, one write-able number: that’s the full service.

The Message Formula

PartWhat it containsExample line
GreetingName if you have it; title if not; never “hey”“Hello Ms. Chen,” • “Hello Riverside Diner Hiring Team,”
Who + whyYour name + the position — one sentence“My name is Alex Rivera and I’m following up on my application for the cashier position.”
One useful factAvailability, a quick interest line, or a question — nothing more“I’m available for an interview weekdays after 3pm.”
CloseThank + contact info“Thank you for your time — you can reach me at 555-0143. I look forward to hearing from you.”

The three message rules

Short: four to six sentences, never a wall of text. Clean: no abbreviations (“idk”, “ngl”, “omg”), no autocorrect disasters — read it aloud before sending. Week 19’s standard: nothing red-zone, nothing you wouldn’t say to their face. Emoji: none, ever, in a first employer message.

Day 3 • You Try It

Message Calls

Which subject line works best for a follow-up email?
The correct one is specific (they know who and what before opening it), professional (no emoji, no all-caps alarm bell). An empty-looking subject gets ignored; a shouting one gets eye-rolls.
A text follow-up comes back with “are you available Tuesday at 10?” You text back…
The first option confirms, thanks, and keeps the professional voice from Day 1. The thumbs-up is for friends. Stalling when you’re available is a pattern that signals you’ll be hard to schedule.
You draft the message and autocorrect changed “position” to “poison.” You hit send before reading. This is a problem because…
Read aloud before sending. No exceptions. The autocorrect rule is permanent: one careful read costs ten seconds; one wrong-word message costs an interview.
You want to stand out in your follow-up message. What’s something specific, short, and professional that would do that — without being over-the-top?
Think first — then click to reveal
Name something concrete from the posting: “I noticed the role involves morning prep — I’m available as early as 6am.” Or tie a skill to the job: “I have experience counting money and making change, which I know matters for a cashier role.” Specific + brief + tied to the job beats generic enthusiasm every time.

Wrap-Up

Week 20 Quick Reference

The story so far

You answer unknown numbers professionally during job search, return calls same day with the position named and number slow, leave voicemails in five parts under 30 seconds with the number twice, and write clean four-to-six-sentence employer messages — read aloud before sending, no abbreviations, nothing red-zone. Three modes, one professional voice.

Keep asking

  • Does my phone voicemail greeting sound professional — have I listened to it lately?
  • What’s my go-to callback window — do I have it memorized for the voicemail leave?
  • Will I read my messages aloud before sending, every time?
