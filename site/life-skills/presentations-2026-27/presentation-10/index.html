Day 1 • Learn

The Check — and the Three Ways to Pay

The enough-money checkExample
1. Total MY money (last week’s skill)Pocket count: $6.50
2. Total the PRICE — and pad for tax: add about a dime per $2 if the tag doesn’t include itTag: $5.99 → plan for about $6.40
3. Compare: money ≥ price?$6.50 vs. ~$6.40 → enough — barely; know it going in

Three ways to pay

Exact amount — build it Hairy-Money style; no change to track. Over and take change — hand a bill bigger than the total; change comes back (tomorrow’s skill). Card — fast, but ONLY works if the money is really in the account; a card is not extra money, it’s YOUR money in disguise.

If the check says not enough: put something back, pick the smaller size, or walk away — all three are power moves. Getting to the register short is the only losing move, and the check makes it impossible.

Day 1 • You Try It

Run the Check

You have $10. Total with tax comes to $9.12. Enough — and how should you pay?
$10 beats $9.12 — the compare step takes one second when your pocket count is already done. Paying over is the right call; tomorrow you’ll know the change is 88¢ before the register does.
Tag says $7.99, tax not included, and you have exactly $8.00. The check says…
The tag isn’t the total — the padding step exists for exactly this trap. One penny of headroom loses to tax every time.
Jaylen’s card got declined for a $6 purchase — the account had $4. What’s the lesson?
Same check, different pocket: know the account total before you rely on it. Cards skip the counting, not the question.
You’re $1 short for the thing you wanted. Name your three power moves — and which one is hardest for you personally?
Think first — then click to reveal
Put something back • pick the smaller/cheaper version • walk away and come back with enough. Most people say walking away is hardest — which is exactly why deciding BEFORE the register, when nobody’s watching, is the pro move.

Read the Receipt, Count Up the Change

Receipt lineIt tells you
Items & pricesWhat you were charged for — check it’s what you bought
SubtotalItems added up, before tax
TaxThe government’s share — the padding you planned for
TOTALThe real number — what you actually pay
Tendered / ChangeWhat you handed over, and what comes back

The count-up strategy (Week 1 veteran, now in its true home)

Count UP from the total to what you paid: total $4.25, paid $5.00 → “$4.25… 4.50, 4.75, 5.00” — that’s 75¢ change, three quarters, known before the drawer opens. Change wrong? Say it politely and immediately: “I think the change should be 75 cents — can we double-check?”

Day 2 • You Try It

Know Your Change First

Total is $3.60. You pay with a $5. Count up — expected change?
Count up to the next dollar first (40¢), then bill-jump to what you paid ($1 more). $1.40 — and you knew it standing in line.
The receipt shows an item you put back on the shelf. Best move?
Registers make mistakes; readers catch them. Polite + immediate + specific fixes it in thirty seconds — that’s what the receipt is FOR.
You expected 75¢ back and got 50¢. The cashier looks busy and the line is long. You…
A quarter today, a dollar next week — your money is your money. Calm and specific isn’t rude; it’s exactly how adults handle it. Count-up gave you the receipt-proof number.
Why learn expected change at all, when the register calculates it and the screen shows it?
Think first — then click to reveal
The register is right until it isn’t — wrong amount keyed in, wrong bills handed back, a scan that ran twice. Knowing YOUR number means machines and mistakes can’t quietly cost you. Checkers get correct change; hopers get whatever comes.
