Day 1 • Learn

The Words on Every Form

The form says…It means…
ApplicantYou — the person applying
EmployerThe business doing the hiring
PositionThe job you want — write the posting’s job title
Legal nameYour full name as it appears on your ID — no nicknames
MIMiddle initial — just the first letter
Current addressWhere you live now — street, city, state, ZIP
N/A“Not applicable” — this question isn’t about me
Signature & dateYour handwritten name promising it’s all true — plus today’s date

The golden rule of forms: never leave a blank. A blank looks like you missed it. N/A shows you read it and it doesn’t apply. Blank = oops; N/A = on purpose.

Day 1 • You Try It

Speak the Language

The form asks for “Position applied for.” You’re applying to Posting A. What do you write?
“Anything” tells them you didn’t read the posting. The exact title tells them you did. Copy it word for word.
Everyone calls you TJ, but your ID says Thomas James Rivera. “Legal name” gets…
Legal name matches your ID and paperwork. Get hired and everyone will happily call you TJ — the form just isn’t the place.
The form asks: “Previous employer.” This is your first job application ever. You write…
N/A is the honest, professional move. Making things up breaks the promise your signature makes at the bottom.
The signature line says: “I certify the above information is true.” In your own words — what are you promising, and why does it matter?
Think first — then click to reveal
You’re promising every line is honest — and employers can check. One made-up answer can cost the job even after you’re hired. Truth on the form protects you later.

A Contact Block, Done Right

CONTACT INFORMATION — the winning version
NameRivera, Thomas J. — last name first when the form says so; read the labels
Address412 Oak Street, Apt 2 — apartment number included, never dropped
City / State / ZIPWinfield, MO 63389 — all three, every time
Phone(636) 555-0142 — ALL ten digits, area code first
Emailtrivera2027@email.com — copied letter-perfect, then checked twice

The three checks

Neat — print, don’t rush; if they can’t read it, it doesn’t exist. Complete — every box, apartment numbers and all. Exact — read your phone and email back one character at a time. This block is how the “you’re hired” call finds you.

Day 2 • You Try It

Spot the Problem

An application lists the email as “trivera2027@emial.com.” What just happened?
Email has no “close enough.” One letter off is a dead end — which is why we read it back character by character.
The phone box has ten spaces. Jordan writes 555-0142 and stops. Problem?
(636) 555-0142 works from anywhere. Ten digits, every time — the boxes are counting for a reason.
Best handwriting strategy for a paper application?
Applications are read by strangers in a hurry. Plain printing that anyone can read beats beautiful writing they have to decode.
Why practice your contact block until it’s automatic — when you could just look everything up each time?
Think first — then click to reveal
You’ll write this block for jobs, doctors, DMV forms, everything — sometimes standing at a counter with no phone allowed. Automatic means fast, calm, and correct anywhere. It’s a skill you keep for life.

Day 3 • Learn

Share • Pause • Protect

ZoneInformationThe rule
SHAREName, phone, email, address, availability, skillsApplications need these — that’s how they reach you
PAUSESocial Security number, birth dateReal employers need these AFTER they hire you, on official tax and ID paperwork — not on a form at a counter or a random website. Unsure? Ask your trusted adult first
PROTECTPasswords, bank or card numbers, moneyNO employer needs these to consider you. Never on an application, never by text, never to “hold your spot”

The scam alarm: real jobs never charge you to apply, never pay you in gift cards, and never rush you to share numbers “right now.” Pressure + secrecy + money = walk away and tell your trusted adult.

Day 3 • You Try It

Sort It: Share, Pause, or Protect?

A paper application at a real store asks for your phone number and email.
If they can’t contact you, they can’t hire you. Contact info on a real application is the point.
An online form you found through a text message wants your Social Security number to “see jobs near you.”
Your SSN belongs on official hiring paperwork AFTER a real job offer — never on a look-around website. Half the digits is still a gift to a thief.
A “hiring manager” texts: “You’re hired! Buy a $100 gift card for your uniform and we’ll pay you back Friday.”
Money flowing FROM the worker TO the “job” is the oldest scam alarm there is. Screenshot it, show your trusted adult, block the number.
Why do scammers target people looking for their FIRST job — and what makes you a hard target now?
Think first — then click to reveal
First-time applicants are excited, hopeful, and unsure what’s normal — scammers count on that. You now know what’s normal: share contact info, pause on SSN until real hiring paperwork, protect money and passwords always. Knowing the zones IS the armor.
