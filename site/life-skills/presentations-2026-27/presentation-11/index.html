The Night Before + The Morning Of

The night before

  • Clothes or uniform laid out — clean, ready, no morning hunt
  • Ride plan confirmed OUT LOUD with your ride — and backup named
  • Know your start time, your supervisor’s name, and where to enter
  • Pack: ID, any paperwork they asked for, water, phone CHARGED (and silenced — Day 3 explains)
  • Sleep — a real bedtime; tired workers make expensive mistakes

The morning of

  • Leave at your ride-plan time — not “around then”
  • Arrive 10–15 minutes early
  • Walk in, find your supervisor, introduce yourself: name + “It’s my first day — where would you like me?”
  • Then the two magic behaviors: listen hard and ask when unsure

Nobody expects a new worker to know the job — they expect them to be on time, prepared, and coachable. All three are decided before you ever clock in.

First-Day Calls

Sam’s first shift starts at 9:00. The bus gets him there at 8:58. Good plan?
First-day timing gets ZERO cushion in that plan. Early bus costs fifteen minutes of waiting; the late arrival costs a first impression that never fully comes back.
You walk in on day one and don’t see your supervisor. Best move?
Knowing the supervisor’s NAME (night-before checklist) turns a scary moment into one easy sentence. Asking is what confident new workers do.
The trainer explains the closing routine fast, and you catch about half. You…
“I want to get it right” is music to a trainer’s ears. Asking on day one is free; guessing on day three costs someone real cleanup.
Why do you think most first-day disasters trace back to the NIGHT BEFORE, not the day itself?
Think first — then click to reveal
Morning-of has no slack: no time to find clothes, charge a phone, or invent a ride. Every night-before item is a morning problem pre-solved. Calm mornings are built, not lucky.

LayerThe ruleWhy it’s a rule
Hygiene — every job, every dayShowered, deodorant, clean teeth, clean hair, trimmed nailsCoworkers and customers share your air all shift — this one is about respect

Dressed Right?

Kennel assistant job, no uniform. Best outfit?
Dress for the DUTIES: active, messy work wants sturdy and washable. “Nice” isn’t the target — RIGHT is.
Your uniform shirt is dirty and your shift is in an hour. You…
Honest beats hidden, and no-showing over laundry turns a small problem into a firing-sized one. Then: night-before checklist gets a “uniform washed” line, permanently.
The non-slip shoes are ugly and your coworker skips them. You…
A kitchen floor doesn’t care about looks. The coworker skipping them is one wet tile from proving why the rule exists.
“Dress for the job” is really about trust. What does a clean, complete, correct appearance tell a boss before you’ve done a single task?
Think first — then click to reveal
It says: I take this seriously, I follow standards without being chased, and you can put me in front of customers. Appearance is the first task every shift — and it’s one you can ace every single day.

The Everyday Three

RuleThe standardThe habit that makes it easy
PhonesSilenced and PUT AWAY during work — pocket, locker, or bag; out only on breaksTell family/friends your shift hours: “I’ll answer at my break at 6.” Emergencies call the workplace phone
BreaksTake them when scheduled, come back ON TIME — a 15 is 15, not 25Note your return time when the break STARTS; set a quiet alarm two minutes early
Staying on taskFinished? Don’t stand there — next task, or the golden question: “What would you like me to do next?”Learn the “always list” of your job: the tasks that are never done (wiping, restocking, sweeping)

Here’s the secret: these three rules are how bosses TELL WORKERS APART. Most people are decent at their tasks — the phone-away, back-on-time, what’s-next worker is the one who gets more hours, better shifts, and the first raise.

Day 3 • You Try It

You Make the Call

Mid-shift, your phone buzzes in your pocket — probably the group chat. You…
“Quick peeks” are never one — and managers clock every single glance. The buzz-proof worker is rarer, and more valuable, than you’d think.
Your 15-minute break started at 2:00. You’re back at…
Ten extra minutes leaves a coworker covering your station — they notice, and so does the schedule-maker. On-time returns are silent reputation builders.
You finish stocking early. The floor by register two is dusty and nobody assigned it. You…
“Finds work without being asked” is the single line managers rave about. The always-list means there IS no “nothing to do.”
A coworker says: “Relax — nobody works the WHOLE shift.” They’re on their phone by the freezer. What do you do — and what do you NOT do?
Think first — then click to reveal
Do: keep your own standard — your habits are your paycheck and your reputation, not theirs. Don’t: lecture them or report every little thing — you’re a coworker, not a boss. Their choices become their problem; yours become your raise.

Wrap-Up

Week 11 Quick Reference

The story so far

You can walk into a first day early, equipped, and introduced — because the night before did the work. You dress and groom for the job, wear every piece of safety gear without a vote, keep the phone away, return from breaks on the clock, and fill slow moments with the golden question. That’s not just first-day behavior — that’s the worker who gets kept.

Keep asking

  • Which night-before item would I actually forget — and where’s my reminder?
  • Can I say my walk-in introduction without rehearsing?
  • What’s on the “always list” for MY best-fit job?

Coming next week — Schedules and Time: reading a weekly work schedule, planning backward from start times, and what to do when you’re running late or absent. The clock becomes your teammate.
