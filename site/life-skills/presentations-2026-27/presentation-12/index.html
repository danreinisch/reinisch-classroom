How to read it — every time

1 Find YOUR name — finger on your row, and stay in your lane. 2 Read across, day by day: “4–8” means start at 4:00, done at 8:00; afternoon shifts are usually PM. 3 OFF means off — a gift, not a suggestion to wander in. 4 COPY your week somewhere you’ll see it — phone or pocket card — the moment it’s posted. 5 Schedules CHANGE: check the board every week, even if “it’s always the same.”

Day 1 • You Try It

Read Alex’s Week

Look at the grid: when does Alex work on Friday?
Finger on Alex’s row, slide to Friday: 5–9. One box over from Thursday’s 4–8 — and one hour later. This is exactly how mix-ups happen without the finger.
How many total shifts does Alex work this week?
Count the boxes with times, skip the OFFs: Tue 4–8, Thu 4–8, Fri 5–9, Sat 8–2. Four shifts — and Saturday starts in the MORNING. Same week, different rhythms.
Jordan glances at the schedule Monday, sees “4-something,” and shows up Tuesday at 4. The grid says Jordan is OFF Tuesday. What went wrong?
Showing up on an OFF day sounds harmless — until it’s the reverse mistake and a shift gets missed. The copy-it-down habit kills both errors at once.
Sam wants to know: “Do I work with Alex at all this week?” Read the grid and settle it.
Think first — then click to reveal
Saturday — both work 8–2. (Thursday they overlap too: Alex 4–8, Sam 4–8.) Reading ACROSS rows answers “when do I work,” reading DOWN columns answers “who works with me.” Both directions are useful.

The Sunday-Night Routine

StepTen minutes, once a weekAlex’s example
1Pocket card out — list every shiftTue 4–8, Thu 4–8, Fri 5–9, Sat 8–2
2Arrive-by time for each (start − 15)3:45 • 3:45 • 4:45 • 7:45
3Leave-home time for each (arrive-by − trip)Trip = 25 min → 3:20 • 3:20 • 4:20 • 7:20
4Flag the ODD one outSaturday MORNING — alarm needed, night-before prep Friday
5Check collisions with lifeFri 5–9 vs. the game? Solve it NOW, not Friday at 4:50

The whole routine is Week 4’s subtraction, four times over — plus one new skill: spotting the odd shift. Late workers are almost never late to their USUAL shift; it’s the weird Saturday morning that gets them. Flag it Sunday and it can’t sneak up.

Day 2 • You Try It

Plan Like Alex

Alex’s trip takes 25 minutes. For the Saturday 8–2 shift, leave-home time is…
Two subtractions, same as always: 8:00 − 15 = 7:45 arrive-by; 7:45 − 25 = 7:20 leave. The habit is the same math on repeat.
Which of Alex’s shifts is the “odd one out” — the one to flag Sunday night?
Three afternoon starts and one early morning — the morning is where autopilot fails. Flagged Sunday = alarm set, clothes out Friday, no 7:40 panic.
Step 5 finds a collision: Alex’s Friday 5–9 shift overlaps a family event at 6. Best move — on SUNDAY?
Early notice is the entire game. Managers can fix almost anything given days — and almost nothing given minutes. (No-showing? Day 4 covers why that’s the one unforgivable.)
Why does the routine live on SUNDAY NIGHT — why not plan each shift the morning of?
Think first — then click to reveal
Morning-of planning meets problems with zero time to solve them — Sunday planning meets them with days. Ten calm minutes once beats five panicked minutes daily. It’s the night-before checklist idea, scaled up to a whole week.

Day 3 • Learn

The Backup Ladder

RungThe planWrite it down as…
Plan AYour normal ride — from the Week 4 planName + leave time (“Mom, leave 3:20”)
Plan BA different PERSON or route, asked in advance“Uncle Ray — said yes, needs 30 min notice”
Plan CThe slow-but-sure way: bus, walk, bike“Bus at 3:05 from Oak St — 40 min”
Weather ruleRain/snow/ice = add time to EVERY rung“Bad weather: leave 15 min earlier, minimum”
The triggerPlan A wobbles → jump rungs EARLY“No confirmation by 2:45 → call Plan B”

The ladder’s secret is the TRIGGER TIME: decide in advance the exact moment you switch plans. Waiting-and-hoping eats the minutes Plan B and C need. Workers with trigger times are never stranded — they’re just occasionally early on the bus.

Day 3 • You Try It

Climb the Ladder

It’s 2:45. Plan A (your ride at 3:20) hasn’t answered two texts. Your trigger time is 2:45. You…
Triggers exist so you don’t negotiate with hope. Calling B “too early” costs an apology; calling too late costs the shift.
Ice storm tonight; tomorrow’s shift starts at 8. Your normal leave time is 7:20. Tonight you…
Weather steals minutes from every plan on the ladder. Adding them the NIGHT BEFORE is free; finding them on ice at 7:25 is impossible.
Why does Plan B need to be someone you’ve ALREADY asked — not just someone you could ask?
Sound familiar? It’s the reference rule from Week 7 wearing a seatbelt: real plans are pre-asked, pre-confirmed, written down.
Alex’s Saturday shift starts at 8am — and Plan C’s first bus doesn’t run until 8:30 on weekends. What does that hole mean for Alex’s ladder, and what would you do about it?
Think first — then click to reveal
Saturday has NO Plan C — which makes Plan B twice as important that day: confirm Uncle Ray Friday night, not Saturday morning. Every shift’s ladder is a little different; checking each rung against each shift is exactly what today’s Do is for.

Day 4 • Do

The Protocol — Then Prove It

The late-or-absent protocol

1. Decide fast — running late or truly can’t come? (Sick enough to stay home is a real answer — and so is “late but coming.”) 2. Call THE WORKPLACE, immediately — a call, not a text to a coworker; the moment you know, not the moment the shift starts. 3. Say the script: who you are, what’s happening, when you’ll arrive (or that you can’t today), and “I’m sorry for the trouble.” 4. Follow through — arrive when you said; on return, ask what you missed. 5. Fix the leak — late because a rung broke? Repair the ladder that night.

Your task Graded scenario set: four situations (overslept for a 4:00 shift and it’s 3:50 • woke up sick at 6am for an 8am shift • bus broke down mid-route • family emergency mid-week). For each, write the protocol response — including the exact phone script, word for word. Then perform ONE of them with me as the manager on the line.

Check out: Your performed call is the exit ticket. Calm, clear, complete — you’ve got this.
