What they’re really trying to find out

Can you do the job? (Skills, experience) • Will you show up? (Reliability, attitude) • Will you fit? (Communication, how you handle people). Every question maps to one of these three. Knowing that turns mystery into a target.

Day 1 • You Try It

Read the Map

The interviewer says “any questions for us?” You have no prepared questions. Best move?
Asking nothing signals low interest. Asking about pay in round one signals you care more about the check than the job — that conversation comes after an offer. A curious question about the role itself signals you’re already thinking about how to do it well.
You arrive ten minutes early. The receptionist says someone will be right with you. You…
Receptionists report back. “They were on their phone the whole wait” has lost real candidates real jobs. The interview starts when you walk in the building — that’s not a cliché, it’s logistics.
Which of the three interviewer questions is “have you ever handled an upset customer?” really asking?
The fit question wears a lot of disguises. “Upset customer,” “difficult coworker,” “biggest mistake” — all of them are checking if you stay calm, stay professional, and treat people well under pressure.
You can only prepare one question to ask them. Which single question gets you the most useful information AND makes the best impression?
Think first — then click to reveal
“What does a successful first 90 days look like in this role?” It shows you’re thinking about doing the job, not just getting it. And the answer tells you exactly what they prioritize — which shapes everything about how you present yourself in the rest of the interview.

The Three-Part Answer

PartWhat it containsExample
1. Who you are — one sentence, work-focusedSchool or current role, relevant to this job“I’m a junior at Winfield High School in a Life Skills program focused on workplace readiness—”
2. What you bring — two receiptsTwo skills from your bank, each with evidence“— I have experience caring for animals — I walked my neighbor’s dogs for two summers — and I’m reliable about schedules, I haven’t missed a school commitment this year—”
3. Why here — one specific lineConnect to THIS job, not jobs in general“— I applied because I’m genuinely interested in working with animals, and Paws & Claws was the first place I looked.”

Total time: 45–60 seconds. Under 30 sounds unready; over 90 sounds like a rehearsal nobody asked for. Two receipts is the magic number: one sounds lucky, three sounds like a list. And the “why here” part is what almost everyone skips — which means the person who does it stands out immediately.

Day 2 • You Try It

Answer Check

Which “tell me about yourself” answer is closest to the three-part structure?
The first: who (Winfield senior), what (aide, never missed a shift), why (training for counter work). The second: no receipts, all claims. The third: life story, zero relevance — a common nervous reflex when there’s no structure underneath.
Your “what you bring” receipt is “I work hard.” The problem?
Every candidate in that waiting room will say “I work hard.” The one who says “I haven’t missed a shift in six months” is saying the same thing — with evidence. Receipts are what separate your skills bank from everyone else’s guess.
You can only pick ONE skill receipt for “what you bring.” Which is the better choice for a kennel assistant role?
Pick receipts that fit the JOB. The dog-care receipt maps directly to kennel work; the algebra grade is irrelevant; the personality line has no receipt. Always choose the two receipts that would make THIS employer nod.
Why does the “why here” part matter so much — can’t the employer assume you’re interested since you applied?
Think first — then click to reveal
Most applicants apply to ten places and have no idea which one just called them. “I applied here because” proves you know where you are and why — and that you’re not just filling a slot. One specific, honest reason (“you’re the closest to my home,” “I pass this place every day,” “I heard you promote from within”) works fine. It just has to be real and specific.

The Receipt Answer Formula

QuestionAnswer structureExample
“What’s your greatest strength?”Name the strength + one receipt + tie to this job“I’m reliable — I haven’t missed a school commitment in two semesters, and in a kennel job I know that matters because the animals don’t wait.”
“Are you reliable?”Yes + a receipt — never just “yes”“Yes — I’ve cared for my neighbor’s dogs every morning for two summers without missing a day, including when it was raining.”
“What can you bring to this team?”Two receipts, tied to the posting’s duties“I follow multi-step directions accurately — I was trusted to run the classroom aide station solo — and I stay calm when things get busy.”

The weakness question — a preview

Almost every interview includes “what’s your greatest weakness?” Next week covers it fully, but the structure is the same: real weakness + what you’re doing about it. “I sometimes rush, so I started using a checklist” is far stronger than “I work too hard.” Receipts even for your weaknesses — because they show you know yourself.

Day 3 • You Try It

Receipt or Claim?

“My greatest strength is that I’m a people person.” Receipt answer or claim?
Every person in that waiting room has said “I’m a people person.” The receipt version makes it real: a role, a duration, a specific outcome (“without a complaint”). Same strength, completely different credibility.
Why does the strength answer need to be TIED TO THIS JOB?
“I’m great at graphic design” at a kennel assistant interview is a curiosity, not a selling point. “I’m reliable — and in a kennel job I know that matters because the animals don’t wait” shows you understand what they’re buying. The tie-in is the pitch.
The interviewer asks “are you reliable?” and you say “yes.” What’s missing?
Plain yes, no receipt: they have to take your word for it. “Yes — I’ve cared for my neighbor’s dogs every morning for two summers” gives them a reason. One sentence of proof is worth ten repetitions of the word reliable.
What’s one receipt from something you ALREADY DO — school, home, community — that proves reliability, and how would you phrase it as an interview answer?
Think first — then click to reveal
Examples: perfect attendance this semester; feeding pets every morning without being reminded; always being at practice or rehearsal; finishing assigned classroom tasks on time every week. The formula: “Yes — I [specific thing] for [how long] without [the failure the employer fears].” Your receipt is already in your real life — today you just gave it a job title.
