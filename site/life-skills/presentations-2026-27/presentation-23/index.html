The Appearance Decision

StepHowExample
1. Read the workplaceLook at their website, walk by, notice employees — before the interviewPaws & Claws employees wear polos and jeans; Riverside Diner wears black uniforms
2. Go one level upInterview dress = one step more polished than the daily uniformCasual workplace → neat jeans + solid shirt; business casual → slacks + button-down
3. The hygiene non-negotiablesClean, no heavy scent, hair controlled, nails clean — always; these override everything elseThe cleanest version of what you own beats expensive clothes with body odor every time
4. Check before you leaveMirror check: stain? wrinkle? lint? tag sticking out?The tag-out fix takes ten seconds; missing it takes the whole interview to shake

What you can’t control vs. what you can

Expensive clothes, a car, a haircut at a salon — not always possible. Clean, ironed or lint-rolled, matching, and smell-neutral — always possible. Interviewers hired in clean thrift-store clothes who showed up five minutes early and said the right things. They didn’t get the job because of the clothes — and they didn’t lose it because of them either.

Day 1 • You Try It

Appearance Decisions

You’re interviewing at a fast-casual restaurant where employees wear black pants and branded t-shirts. Your interview look should be…
One level up says “I took this seriously” without overdoing it. A suit at a fast-casual interview reads as out of touch; showing up in the uniform you guessed at reads as presumptuous. The step-up is always the right calibration.
Your nicest outfit has a wrinkle across the front. You notice it when you put it on. You have 30 minutes before you need to leave. You…
This is exactly why the Week 11 night-before checklist exists: lay it out the night before, catch the wrinkle then. But 30 minutes is real fix time — the steam trick takes five, ironing takes ten. The interview notices things you think they won’t.
Which hygiene choice matters most, even above the outfit?
You can overcome a casual outfit; you cannot overcome an overwhelming smell. Smell-neutral is the absolute floor — clean skin, clean clothes, no heavy spray. Everything else is a grace note on top of that foundation.
You genuinely can’t afford interview clothes right now. Build a realistic plan using what you DO have access to — no invented resources.
Think first — then click to reveal
Most people have or can borrow: one clean solid-color shirt or blouse, one pair of dark pants or jeans with no holes, clean shoes. Add: lint roller (dollar store), iron or steam option, nail clean-up. Other options: school counselor (often has a clothes closet), thrift store ($5–10), a family member’s one-day borrow. The goal is clean + intentional, not expensive — and that goal is almost always reachable.

The Greeting Sequence

MomentWhat you doThe detail that counts
They extend the handFirm, dry, two pumps, release — mirror their strengthLimp = unconfident; bone-crushing = aggressive; both are remembered
They introduce themselves“Nice to meet you, [their name].” Use the name once, right awayNames from Week 14 — saying it once cements it in YOUR memory too
They offer you a seatSit upright, slightly forward — feet on floor, hands visibleSlouch = bored; crossed arms = closed; hands under the table = hiding
During the interviewEye contact 60–70% — enough to be engaged, not a stare-down; nod when they finishLooking away = distracted; looking down = unconfident; looking at your notes = the notes run the interview
They say somethingWait one beat before answering — pause is thinking, not emptinessRushing = nervous; pausing = confident. One beat is all it takes.

The handshake rule: match-then-steady. If they’re formal, go formal; if they’re relaxed, relax slightly — but always firm enough to feel like a real handshake. The one-beat pause before answering is the most underrated interview skill: it separates the person who prepared from the person who’s panicking through their answers.

Day 2 • You Try It

First Impression Calls

You sit down and immediately cross your arms because the chair is cold. What does the interviewer read?
The interviewer can’t see inside your head — they see the signal. Slightly forward, hands on the table or lap, is the default that reads engaged and open. Cold? uncross slowly after the first minute when you’re warmed up.
The interviewer asks a question you weren’t expecting. You pause for two seconds before answering. What does that communicate?
The one-beat pause is one of the most powerful interview moves, and almost no one uses it. Instant answers sound rehearsed or panicked; a brief pause sounds like someone who thinks before speaking — which is exactly who they want to hire.
You don’t know their name because you missed it when they introduced themselves. You…
Asking once, politely, is professional. Avoiding names the whole interview is obvious. Guessing wrong — “so as I was saying, Rachel—” when it’s Rebecca — is the worst option. Asking shows you care about getting it right.
How does the Week 14 tone-dial framework apply specifically to the moment the interviewer says “tell me about yourself” and you begin your answer?
Think first — then click to reveal
Volume: indoor, steady — nerves tend to push it either too quiet (mumble) or too loud (nervous projection). Speed: slow down 10% from your normal pace — rushed answers sound panicked. Face & body: eyes up, slight lean forward, hands visible and still. The three dials are pre-set from Day 2 of this unit — today you aim them at the specific moment they matter most.

The Full Interview Window

WhenWhat you doWhat it signals
Parking lot / entrancePhone away and silent; walk in calmlySomeone may see you. Your face during the walk is your resting state.
Reception“Hello, I’m [name] — I have an interview with [contact] at [time].” Warm, clear.Receptionists report back. Always.
WaitingPhone away, seated upright, light look around — observe the environmentPhone-out while waiting = the visual the interviewer sees first when they come out
After the interviewThank by name, firm close: “Thank you, [name] — I’m looking forward to hearing from you.” Handshake if offered.The close is often what they write down in the notes
After you leaveOptional thank-you text or email within 24 hours: Week 20’s message formulaAlmost nobody does this. The ones who do are remembered.

The full window runs from the moment you pull into the parking lot to the moment the thank-you message arrives. Most candidates only think about the questions in the middle. You now own both ends — which is where the decision often actually gets made.

Day 3 • You Try It

Full Window Checks

You arrive 5 minutes early and sit in the waiting area. While waiting, you…
Even reviewing your notes on your phone looks the same as scrolling to the person walking out to get you. The answer bank is in your head now — that’s why Week 22’s Day 4 was a practice day. The waiting room is for breathing, not reviewing.
The interview ends. The interviewer shakes your hand. Your close is…
The close with the name is the move. Asking for the offer directly puts them on the spot (not good). Rushing out undersells the whole interview. One sentence, their name, a genuine look — it’s the last frame and it has to hold.
Why does sending a thank-you message within 24 hours help you — even for a simple entry-level job?
Brief, professional, specific to the interview: “Thank you for your time today — the kennel assistant role sounds like exactly the kind of work I’ve been building toward. I hope to hear from you soon.” Four sentences, Week 20’s formula, sent within 24 hours. Rare. Remembered.
Map the full interview window for a real interview this week — when does it start for you, what are the pressure points, and what’s your close line?
Think first — then click to reveal
Start time = when you leave home (appearance check done, phone silenced). Pressure points: walking in, waiting (phone away), TMAY first answer. Close line: “Thank you, [name] — I’m looking forward to hearing from you.” Post-interview: thank-you message within 24 hours. The window you control is wider than most people realize — and now you have a plan for all of it.
