Before We Begin

What “Low-Pressure” Means

This IS

  • A short practice with three to five questions
  • Permission to use a prepared answer sheet
  • More than one valid way to communicate
  • Specific feedback and a second try
  • A chance to show growth at your support level

This is NOT

  • A surprise interrogation
  • A contest for the fastest speaker
  • A requirement to make eye contact or shake hands
  • A grade for being nervous
  • A full-length formal interview

You may answer verbally, read or point to a prepared response, use sentence frames, type a response, or use another teacher-approved communication method. The skill is communicating a useful answer — not pretending everyone communicates the same way.

The Success Target

One Improvement Is the Win

We will notice

  • Preparation: answer sheet is ready
  • Communication: response answers the question
  • Professional behavior: greeting, listening, and closing
  • Use of support: student uses tools appropriately
  • Growth: one change after feedback

Teacher evidence

Record completion, response accuracy, independence level from 0–5, and the prompts or supports used. A supported success is still a success; the support level tells us what to teach next.

Weekly goal: Participate in a brief supported mock interview and use feedback to improve one response or behavior.

Day 1 • Learn

Answer Cues, Not Full Scripts

Useful cue card

Strength: dependable
Proof: classroom job every day
Result: materials ready on time

The cues help you remember your own answer while still sounding like a person.

Overloaded script

A full paragraph in tiny print that must be read word for word. If you lose your place, the whole answer disappears.

A script may be appropriate for some students, but start with short cues whenever they are enough.

Turn this answer into three short cues: “I am organized because I check our classroom supply shelf every afternoon, restock what is low, and tell the teacher when we need more.”
Think first — then reveal
Organized • check and restock shelf • report low supplies. Three cues preserve the evidence without forcing the speaker to memorize every word.

Day 1 • Hours and Pay

Answer Only What You Know

The posting lists $14 per hour. The interviewer asks, “Is the listed pay acceptable?”
Use the information provided and answer respectfully. This practice is not asking students to negotiate unknown pay.
You can work after school, but you need a ride and cannot arrive before 4:30. What is the accurate answer?
Accurate availability protects both the worker and employer. Do not promise hours or transportation you cannot reliably provide.

Day 1 • Do

Build the Preparation Sheet

Your task Choose a real or class-provided entry-level job. Prepare cues for three to five interview questions. Include accurate availability and the listed pay only when those details are provided. Mark the support you plan to use: choices, word bank, frames, cue card, typed response, rehearsal, extra wait time, or independent response.

Ready check

  • I know which job I am discussing.
  • My cues answer the actual questions.
  • My availability is honest.
  • I chose a support that helps without doing the answer for me.

Practice choice

Read the cues silently, say the answers to a partner or teacher, record yourself, type the responses, or rehearse using your communication system.

Before you leave: Show the teacher your preparation sheet and identify the question you most need to rehearse.

Day 2 • Interviewer Card

Ask Clearly and Consistently

Opening

“Hello. Thank you for meeting with me. I’m going to ask you [three/four/five] questions about the [job title] position. You may use your preparation sheet.”

Question set

  1. Tell me about yourself.
  2. Why do you want this job?
  3. What is one strength you would bring?
  4. Tell me about a problem or feedback.
  5. When are you available?

Closing

“Thank you. Do you have one question for me?”

Then: “That completes our practice interview.”

Interviewer rules

  • Wait at least five seconds.
  • Repeat once when requested.
  • Do not correct during the answer.
  • Save feedback until the round ends.

Day 2 • Candidate Card

Listen → Pause → Answer

The three-step pattern

  1. Listen to the whole question.
  2. Pause long enough to choose your cue.
  3. Answer the question with one useful example when possible.

If you get stuck

  • Ask for the question again.
  • Ask for different wording.
  • Check the prepared cue.
  • Choose from teacher-provided options.
  • Say, “I need a moment to think.”
Professional greeting: Use a clear verbal greeting or another agreed-upon professional greeting. A handshake is optional and should never be required.

Feedback Is a Direction

  1. What is the difference between feedback about a response and a judgment about a person?

Keep → Change → Retry

StepQuestion to askExample
KEEPWhat already worked?“My answer was clear and honest.”
CHANGEWhat one specific thing will improve it?“I will add an example that proves I am dependable.”
RETRYCan I show the change now?Answer the same question again using the example.

Feedback sentence

“Keep ____________. Change ____________. On the retry, try ____________.”

Do not change five things at once. One target makes improvement visible and gives the student a fair chance to succeed.

Day 3 • You Try It

Which Feedback Can You Use?

The candidate says only, “I’m responsible.” Which feedback creates a useful retry?
The first option preserves what worked, names one observable change, and tells the candidate exactly what to retry.
The candidate gives a good answer but begins before the interviewer finishes the question. The best target is…
The answer does not need replacing. The retry target is listening and timing.
A student used a cue card successfully. What should the feedback say?
Supports are part of the evidence. The goal is effective communication with the appropriate level of independence.

Day 3 • The Retry Ladder

Choose the Smallest Useful Retry

Response targets

  • Add one real example.
  • Answer the actual question.
  • Shorten a rambling answer.
  • Use accurate availability.
  • Replace an invented detail with an honest one.

Behavior targets

  • Listen through the whole question.
  • Ask for repetition or clarification.
  • Use the prepared support.
  • Begin with a professional greeting.
  • End with a clear closing.

A retry may be verbal, typed, recorded, read from revised cues, or completed with another approved communication method.

Day 4 • Choose Your Response Level

Same Reflection, Different Supports

Choice / supported response

Select or point to a strength, evidence example, and next step. Complete verbally or with a teacher-recorded response.

Sentence frames

“One strength I showed was ____. I know because ____. Next time I will ____. The support that helped was ____.”

Independent response

Explain one strength with evidence, describe the retry improvement, choose one next step, and state whether the current support should stay, decrease, or change.

Extension

Explain how the next step would improve a future real interview and identify a realistic way to practice it.

Wrap-Up

Week 24 Quick Reference

The practice cycle

Prepare: preview questions and select supports.
Participate: complete three to five questions.
Feedback: keep one strength and change one target.
Retry: make the improvement visible.
Reflect: name evidence and the next step.

Keep asking

  • Did my response answer the actual question?
  • What real example proves my answer?
  • Which support helped me communicate?
  • What one change matters most next time?

Next week: work schedules, timecards, and hours. Interview practice turns into another employment reality — reading when you work and checking whether the recorded hours are correct.
