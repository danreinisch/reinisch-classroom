Honest + Specific = Strong

Answer typeExampleWhat it signals
Generic“I’m a hard worker and I want to gain experience.”You didn’t read the posting; they’re a backup
Only money“I need a job.”You’ll leave the second something better appears
Honest + specific“I walk past here every day and I’ve always liked that it’s clean and busy. I want my first job to be somewhere I actually like being.”You know where you are and you chose it on purpose
Honest + skill-tied“I’ve been caring for animals at home and with neighbors for two years. A kennel job is a real next step, and I looked up Paws & Claws specifically because you take walk-ins.”You did research; you connect the dots

The formula is simple: one real reason + one detail that shows you know where you are. The detail doesn’t have to be impressive — “you’re on the bus route” plus “and I want steady hours” is more compelling than a fake passion for food service delivery metrics. They can feel the difference.

Day 1 • You Try It

Why This Job?

Which “why this job” answer is most likely to move an interviewer?
Option A: real experience + specific detail about THIS employer. It tells the story without performing it. Options B and C tell the interviewer nothing that distinguishes you from the ten people before you.
You honestly don’t have a strong reason — you applied because it was close. How do you handle that?
Honest proximity + one real observation (“clean and busy”) is both true and compelling. Fake passion is detectable and embarrassing when followed up. Saying “I don’t know” is forfeit.
Why does “I need a job” hurt you even though the interviewer knows it’s true?
Honesty wins — but honest AND specific wins more. Employers hire people who want the job, not just a job. Even one detail that shows you looked at them specifically changes the signal completely.
Build a “why this job” answer for a counter server role at the school cafeteria — using real reasons someone like you might actually have.
Think first — then click to reveal
“I eat here every day and I like how fast the line moves — I want to know what that looks like from the other side. And it’s on campus, which solves the transportation problem for now.” That’s honest + specific + shows you noticed something real. No fake passion required.

1. Your moveWhat you’d do FIRST — specific action“I’d stop what I’m doing, give them full attention, and let them finish—”

Scenario Checks

“What would you do if you made a mistake at work?” Best answer shape?
Option A: three-step structure (own, fix, prevent) plus a real receipt. Option B: a red flag — everyone makes mistakes; that answer proves you either lie or don’t notice. Option C: too vague to be convincing.
“What would you do if a coworker asked you to cover their shift but you’re already scheduled?”
The right answer shows you understand workplace process, not just people-pleasing or rule-following. Manager-in-the-loop is always the right step for schedule changes — and saying so shows you get how jobs actually work.
Your scenario answer is 3 minutes long. The problem?
Scenario answers should be shorter than they feel. The three-step structure naturally caps them: your move, your limit, tie back. Three tight sentences beats a three-minute stream of consciousness — every time.
Pick one scenario question. Map it to the Life Skills SC week that taught you the answer — and build the three-step response from that lesson.

Example: “What would you do if a customer was upset?” → Week 17 (coworkers/customers) + Week 15 (asking for help when it’s above your level). Move: listen fully, calm tone. Limit: handle what’s mine, get manager for money/complaints. Tie: “I learned staying calm makes the situation shorter.” Your whole school year is a scenario prep course.

Honest + Protective = Strong

QuestionAnswer structureExample
“When are you available?”Specific days and hours from your real grid — never “anytime”“Weekdays after 3:15pm, and I’m open Saturday and Sunday mornings from 8am on.”
“Any limitations we should know about?”State what’s relevant + what you can do — never over-explain or apologize“I use a communication support for reading long instructions — for task work I follow directions accurately and I check in to confirm I’ve got it right.”
“Can you work weekends?”Yes/no + specific, never vague“Saturday mornings yes, Sunday depends on my family’s schedule — I can confirm weekly.”

You are not required to disclose a disability

That’s the law. You ARE required to tell them what you can and can’t do for the job — those are different conversations. If you have a support that helps you do the job BETTER, you can frame it that way: “I use a checklist to stay accurate on multi-step tasks — I find it keeps me from rushing.” That’s not disclosure; that’s a strength.

Day 3 • You Try It

Availability Calls

You’re free after 3pm weekdays and both weekend days. You say “anytime.” What’s the risk?
Week 4 and Week 12 both said this: honest availability protects the job. “Anytime” is the availability trap. “After 3pm weekdays and weekends” sets you up for shifts you can actually cover — and that’s what reliability actually looks like.
The interviewer asks about limitations. You use a text-to-speech app for reading. Which response works best?
Option A: names the tool, leads with the outcome (accurate, confirms). Option B: over-shares medical framing the job doesn’t require. Option C: a mismatch they’ll discover on day one. Frame what you USE and what it produces — not the diagnosis behind it.
You genuinely ARE available anytime — you have no school schedule conflicts, no other commitments. What’s the best way to say it?
If it’s true, say it specifically — “mornings, evenings, weekends” is specific AND enthusiastic. A shrug reads as uninterested; a list is unnecessary. Specificity = credibility even when the answer is “flexible.”
Build your real availability statement in interview language — using your actual school schedule as the starting point, just like Week 4.
Think first — then click to reveal
Template: “I’m in school weekdays until [time], so I’m available weekdays after [time + 15 min buffer]. [Weekend situation honestly.] I can confirm my schedule in advance each week.” The buffer and the confirmation offer both make you look like someone who has THOUGHT about showing up — which is exactly the reliability signal they’re reading for.
