Catch It, Count It, Say It Back

MoveHowExample
1. Catch — full stop, eyes upStop your hands, face the speaker; directions given to your back bounce offPut the broom down for ten seconds
2. Count — how many steps?Listen for the joints: “then,” “after that,” “next,” “when you’re done”“Empty, reline, THEN boxes, THEN sweep” = 4
3. Say it back — ten seconds“Got it — empty and reline the bins, break down boxes, sweep the back?”Wrong? They fix it NOW, free of charge
4. Anchor — if it’s 3+ stepsFingers, a pocket note, or ask for it written — all professional movesFour steps = four fingers; drop none

Say-it-back feels awkward for exactly one week — then it becomes the thing trainers praise you for. Ten seconds of echo saves twenty minutes of redoing, and NOBODY was ever fired for confirming directions.

Day 1 • You Try It

Count the Steps

“Wipe the tables, then flip the chairs up, and when you’re done, grab me from the office.” How many steps?
The joints give it away. Miss the count and the last step (“grab me”) is the one that quietly vanishes — and it’s the one the manager is waiting on.
Mid-task, you realize you remember steps 1 and 2 but step 3 is… gone. Best move?
Naming what you HAVE done shows the directions mostly landed — asking for the missing piece is recovery, not failure. Guessing is how the wrong shelf gets reorganized.
The manager rattles off five steps while you’re elbow-deep in a task. What’s the FIRST move?
Directions delivered to a working back have a survival rate near zero. Ten seconds of full attention is cheaper than one redone task — managers know this and respect the pause.
Why is say-it-back BETTER than just saying “got it” — from the MANAGER’S side of the conversation?
Think first — then click to reveal
“Got it” tells the manager nothing — everyone says it, including people who got nothing. The echo PROVES what landed, lets them fix errors in ten seconds, and marks you as someone they can hand bigger jobs to. It’s a two-way receipt.

Day 2 • Learn

Checklist Rules — and a Real One

CLOSING CHECKLIST — Snack Counter

  • ☐ Wipe all counters with sanitizer
  • ☐ Restock cups, lids, napkins
  • ☐ Empty both trash bins & reline
  • ☐ Sweep behind the counter
  • ☐ Lock the register drawer
  • ☐ Lights off, sign flipped

The four rules

1. Read it ALL first — thirty seconds of preview beats surprises at step five.

2. Check as you GO — the mark happens when the task finishes, not in a batch at the end. Batch-checking is how “done” lies.

3. In doubt? It’s unchecked — “pretty sure I restocked” means go look.

4. Done = every box + a last look — sweep the list top to bottom once more before you call it.

A checklist isn’t training wheels — it’s professional equipment. The worker WITH the list closes perfectly on their worst day; the worker without it closes perfectly only on their best.

Day 2 • You Try It

Checklist Calls

You’re on step 4 (sweep) and can’t remember if you checked step 2 (restock) or just meant to. Rule?
Thirty seconds of walking beats a morning shift finding zero cups. The rule exists precisely for the “pretty sure” feeling.
A coworker finishes closing, then sits down and checks all six boxes at once from memory. What’s the problem?
The whole power of a checklist is that the mark and the task happen together. Six checks from the couch is just a memory test — the thing checklists exist to replace.
The closing list says “lock the register drawer,” but the manager already took the drawer to the office. You…
A checklist can be TRUE without every box checked — the note says exactly why. That’s N/A’s cousin from Week 5: never a silent skip.
Day 1 taught catching SPOKEN directions; today is WRITTEN ones. When would you convert one into the other — and how?
Think first — then click to reveal
Any spoken direction with 3+ steps deserves to become a pocket checklist — that’s the “anchor” move graduating. “Empty, reline, boxes, sweep” scribbled on a pad IS a checklist. The best workers are constantly turning air into paper.

Day 3 • Learn

The Two Ordering Questions

QuestionWhat it catchesExample
“What does this step NEED?”Steps that depend on earlier stepsRelining bins NEEDS empty bins → empty first
“What does this step RUIN?”Steps that undo later work if done earlySweeping before box breakdown = cardboard bits on a clean floor → sweep LAST

The dish-room proof

Task: “Wash the dishes, clear the tables, wipe the tables, take out the trash.” Run the questions: washing NEEDS the dishes → clear first. Wiping RUINS nothing but needs cleared tables → after clearing. Trash collects everything → last. True order: clear → wash → wipe → trash — not the order it was said in. Spoken order is often just the order someone THOUGHT of the steps.

Messy-to-clean is the master pattern: do the messy steps first, the cleaning steps last, and the “lock up” steps very last. When in doubt, ask yourself which step would be ruined — or ask the manager: “Do you want A or B first?” (Week 15 will make that question famous.)

Day 3 • You Try It

Order Up

Task steps: mop the floor • stack the chairs on tables • sweep. Correct order?
Sweeping NEEDS a clear floor (chairs up); mopping RUINS nothing but is ruined by sweeping after it. The two questions solve it in seconds.
The manager says: “Restock the shelf and break down the delivery boxes.” Which first — and why?
“What does breakdown ruin?” — full boxes. The stock comes OUT, then the cardboard goes flat. Spoken order ≠ task order, again.
You genuinely can’t tell whether the display should be built before or after the floor gets waxed. Best move?
The two questions solve MOST orders — and the ask solves the rest. A specific either/or question makes you look sharp, not lost.
Why does “messy first, clean last, lock-up very last” work as a master pattern for almost every closing routine?
Think first — then click to reveal
Because cleaning is the step everything else RUINS — every messy task after it undoes it. Putting all mess-makers first protects the cleaners, and lock-up last means nothing needs reopening. One pattern, a hundred tasks.

Start to Finish

Your task I’ll give your station’s directions OUT LOUD, once — five steps, deliberately out of true order. You: catch, count, say it back, anchor it into a written checklist, re-order it with the two questions, then DO the task checking as you go. Stations are real classroom work (sorting, assembling, organizing, resetting).

Full credit looks like

An echo that matched • a written checklist in true order (not spoken order!) • boxes checked as-you-went • a finished station • and the Week 11 closer: “Done — what would you like me to do next?”

Check out: Walk me through your checklist — where did your order differ from the order I said it in, and why?
