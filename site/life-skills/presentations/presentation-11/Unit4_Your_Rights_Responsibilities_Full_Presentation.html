Employee Rights

What the law says your employer MUST give you

What Are Employee Rights?

Employee rights are things the law says you're entitled to at work. Your employer MUST follow these rules — they're not optional.

⚖️ The law protects you. There are federal laws (for the whole country) and state laws (for your state) that protect workers.

Your basic rights as an employee include:

  • Getting paid at least minimum wage
  • Getting breaks during your shift
  • Working in a safe environment
  • Not being discriminated against
  • Not being harassed
  • Getting paid for all hours you work

Knowing your rights helps you recognize when something isn't right — and know what to do about it.

Right #1: Minimum Wage

The law says employers must pay you at least a minimum wage — the lowest amount they're legally allowed to pay per hour.

As of 2024:

  • Federal minimum wage: $7.25/hour
  • Missouri minimum wage: $12.30/hour
  • Your employer must pay whichever is HIGHER

Important notes:

  • Some jobs (like servers) can pay less IF you earn tips — but your total must still equal minimum wage
  • Some states have higher minimum wages than others
  • Your employer can NEVER pay you less than minimum wage

🚨 If you're being paid less than minimum wage, that's illegal. You can report it to the Department of Labor.

Right #2: Breaks

Most states require employers to give workers breaks during their shifts.

In Missouri:

  • Workers under 16 must get a 30-minute break if working 5+ hours
  • For adults, Missouri doesn't require breaks by law — but most employers give them anyway
  • If your employer SAYS you get a break, they must let you take it

What most employers provide:

  • One 10-15 minute paid break for shifts 4-6 hours
  • One 30-minute unpaid meal break for shifts 6+ hours
  • Two short breaks plus one meal break for 8-hour shifts

⚠️ If your employer promises breaks in their handbook or when they hire you, they must honor that promise.

Right #3: A Safe Workplace

Your employer is required by law to provide a safe place to work. This is enforced by a government agency called OSHA (Occupational Safety and Health Administration).

🛡️
Your employer MUST:
  • Keep the workplace free of serious hazards
  • Provide safety training
  • Provide safety equipment (gloves, goggles, etc.) if needed
  • Post safety information where you can see it
  • Keep records of work-related injuries
✋
You have the right to:
  • Report unsafe conditions without being punished
  • Refuse work that puts you in immediate danger
  • Request an OSHA inspection if your workplace is unsafe

Right #4: No Discrimination

Discrimination means treating someone unfairly because of who they are — not because of how they do their job.

It's ILLEGAL to discriminate based on:

  • Race or skin color
  • Religion
  • Sex or gender
  • National origin (where you or your family are from)
  • Age (40 and older)
  • Disability
  • Pregnancy
  • Genetic information

🔹 Examples of discrimination:

  • Not hiring someone because of their religion
  • Paying women less than men for the same job
  • Firing someone because they got pregnant
  • Not promoting someone because of their race

Right #5: Get Paid for All Hours Worked

If you work, you must be paid. It's that simple.

Your employer MUST pay you for:

  • All hours you're clocked in and working
  • Time spent in required training
  • Time spent in required meetings
  • Overtime (time over 40 hours/week) at 1.5x your regular rate

These things are ILLEGAL:

  • Asking you to work "off the clock"
  • Not paying you for all your hours
  • Changing your time card to show fewer hours
  • Not paying overtime when you've earned it

💡 Keep your own records. Write down when you start and end each day. If there's ever a dispute, you'll have proof.

Day 1 Review: Employee Rights

🤔 Think About It #1:

Your friend says their boss pays them $6.00 per hour. Is this legal?

Answer: No! That's below both federal ($7.25) and Missouri ($12.30) minimum wage. This is illegal.

🤔 Think About It #2:

Your boss asks you to stay 15 minutes after your shift to finish cleaning, but says "don't clock those minutes." Is this okay?

Answer: No. If you work, you must be paid. This is called "off the clock" work and it's illegal.

🤔 Think About It #3:

You notice the emergency exit at your job is blocked by boxes. What's your right?

Answer: You have the right to a safe workplace. You can report this to your manager, and if nothing changes, you can report it to OSHA.

Employee Responsibilities

What YOUR job requires of YOU

Rights Come With Responsibilities

Just like you have rights at work, you also have responsibilities — things you're expected to do as an employee.

When you accept a job, you're agreeing to:

  • Show up when scheduled
  • Do the work you were hired to do
  • Follow the rules and policies
  • Be honest
  • Treat others with respect
  • Represent the company appropriately

🤝 Think of it as a deal: Your employer gives you rights (pay, safety, fair treatment). In return, you give them your best work and follow the rules.

Responsibility #1: Show Up & Be On Time

We've talked about this before, but it's so important it's worth repeating.

Your responsibilities:

  • Know your schedule
  • Arrive on time (or early) for every shift
  • Call in properly if you're sick
  • Request time off in advance
  • Don't leave early without permission

Why this matters:

When you don't show up, other people have to do your work. It affects your coworkers, your boss, and the customers. Being reliable is one of the most basic — and most important — responsibilities you have.

Responsibility #2: Do Your Job

This sounds obvious, but it means more than just showing up.

"Doing your job" means:

  • Completing the tasks you're assigned
  • Doing work the way you're trained to do it
  • Working at a reasonable pace — not too slow
  • Asking questions if you don't understand something
  • Paying attention to quality, not just finishing fast
  • Staying focused — not on your phone or chatting all day

⚠️ If you consistently don't do your job well, your employer has the right to let you go. Just like they have to follow the law, you have to do what you were hired to do.

Responsibility #3: Follow Rules & Policies

Every workplace has rules. Following them is part of your job.

Common rules you must follow:

  • Dress code
  • Phone/social media policy
  • Break policies
  • Safety rules
  • Clocking in/out procedures
  • How to handle money, food, or products

Breaking rules can lead to:

  • Verbal warning
  • Written warning
  • Suspension
  • Termination (getting fired)

💡 If you think a rule is unfair, you can talk to your manager about it respectfully. But until it changes, you still need to follow it.

Responsibility #4: Be Honest

Honesty is one of the most important things employers look for. Once you lose their trust, it's very hard to get it back.

✅ Being Honest:

  • Admitting when you make a mistake
  • Clocking your actual hours
  • Telling the truth about why you called in
  • Reporting problems you notice
  • Not taking things that aren't yours

❌ Dishonesty:

  • Lying about being sick
  • Having someone clock in for you
  • Stealing products, food, or money
  • Blaming others for your mistakes
  • Lying on your time card

🚨 Dishonesty often leads to immediate termination — no warnings, just fired. It's not worth the risk.

Responsibility #5: Treat Others With Respect

You're responsible for how you treat your coworkers, your boss, and customers.

Respectful behavior includes:

  • Speaking politely to everyone
  • Not gossiping or spreading rumors
  • Not bullying, threatening, or harassing anyone
  • Listening when others are talking
  • Accepting feedback without arguing
  • Helping coworkers when you can

Disrespectful behavior can get you fired:

  • Yelling at coworkers or customers
  • Making rude or offensive comments
  • Creating a hostile environment
  • Threatening anyone

💡 The Golden Rule applies at work: Treat others the way you want to be treated.

Day 2 Review: Employee Responsibilities

🤔 Think About It #1:

Your coworker is always on their phone and not doing their work. You end up having to do extra. Is this fair?

Answer: No — your coworker isn't meeting their responsibility to do their job. Focus on your own work, and if it keeps affecting you, you can talk to your manager.

🤔 Think About It #2:

You accidentally break a piece of equipment. What should you do?

Answer: Tell your manager honestly. Accidents happen. Lying about it or hiding it is worse than the accident itself.

🤔 Think About It #3:

Name 3 responsibilities every employee has.

Answer: Show up on time, do your job, follow the rules, be honest, treat others with respect. (Any 3 of these!)

Paychecks & Taxes

Understanding your pay stub and why money comes out

What Is a Pay Stub?

A pay stub (also called a paycheck stub or earnings statement) is a document that shows how much you earned and what was taken out.

You get a pay stub every time you're paid. It might be:

  • A paper attached to your check
  • Available online through a payroll website
  • Emailed to you

Important terms to know:

  • Gross Pay: The total amount you earned BEFORE anything is taken out
  • Deductions: Money taken out of your paycheck
  • Net Pay: The amount you actually take home (gross pay minus deductions)

⚠️ Your net pay will ALWAYS be less than your gross pay because of taxes and other deductions. This is normal!

Reading a Pay Stub

PAY STUB — BURGER PALACE

Employee: Jake Smith Pay Period: Jan 1–14
EARNINGS
Regular Hours: 35 hrs × $13.00$455.00
Overtime Hours: 0$0.00
GROSS PAY$455.00
DEDUCTIONS
Federal Income Tax−$34.13
State Income Tax (MO)−$18.20
Social Security (FICA)−$28.21
Medicare−$6.60
TOTAL DEDUCTIONS−$87.14
NET PAY (Take Home)$367.86

Jake earned $455 but takes home $367.86. The difference ($87.14) went to taxes.

Why Do They Take Money Out?

The money that comes out of your paycheck is called deductions. Most deductions are for taxes.

Common deductions:

DeductionWhat It's For
Federal Income TaxPays for the federal government (military, roads, etc.)
State Income TaxPays for your state government (schools, police, etc.)
Social Security (FICA)Retirement benefits for when you're older
MedicareHealth insurance for people 65 and older

💡 Everyone pays these taxes — it's the law. The amount depends on how much you earn.

What Is a W-2?

At the end of each year, your employer gives you a form called a W-2. You need this to file your taxes.

The W-2 shows:

  • How much money you earned that year (total wages)
  • How much was taken out for federal taxes
  • How much was taken out for state taxes
  • How much was taken out for Social Security and Medicare

Important W-2 facts:

  • Employers must send W-2s by January 31 each year
  • You need it to file your tax return
  • Keep it in a safe place!
  • If you worked multiple jobs, you'll get a W-2 from EACH employer

Filing Your Taxes

Every year, you have to file a tax return. This is how you report your income to the government.

Tax filing basics:

  • Deadline: Usually April 15 each year
  • What you need: Your W-2(s) and personal information
  • How to file: Online (free options available), tax software, or a tax preparer

You might get money BACK!

If too much tax was taken out of your paychecks during the year, you get a tax refund. Many people get money back when they file!

🔹 Free tax filing options:

  • IRS Free File (irs.gov) — if you earn under ~$79,000
  • VITA (Volunteer Income Tax Assistance) — free help in your community

Check Your Pay Stub!

It's your responsibility to make sure your pay stub is correct.

Every pay period, check:

  • Hours worked: Does it match what you actually worked?
  • Pay rate: Is it the correct hourly rate?
  • Overtime: Did you get 1.5x for hours over 40?
  • Deductions: Do the amounts look similar to last time?

If something looks wrong:

  • Talk to your manager or HR department right away
  • Show them your own records of hours worked
  • Mistakes happen — but they need to be fixed

💡 Keep your pay stubs! Save them (paper or digital) for at least a year. You might need them for taxes, loans, or if there's a dispute.

Answer: It was taken out for deductions — mostly taxes (federal, state, Social Security, Medicare).

🤔 Think About It #2:

Answer: Your W-2 form, which your employer must give you by January 31.

🤔 Think About It #3:

Answer: Talk to your manager or HR right away. Show them your own records if you have them. This needs to be fixed.

Workplace Discrimination & Harassment

What it is, what to do, and who can help

What Is Discrimination?

Discrimination at work means treating someone unfairly because of who they are — not because of their job performance.

It's illegal to discriminate based on:

  • Race, color, or ethnicity
  • Religion
  • Sex, gender, or pregnancy
  • National origin (where you're from)
  • Age (if you're 40 or older)
  • Disability

🔹 Examples of discrimination:

  • Not getting hired because of your race
  • Being paid less because of your gender
  • Being fired because you're pregnant
  • Not getting promoted because of your religion
  • Being harassed because of where you're from

What Is Harassment?

Harassment is unwanted behavior that makes the workplace hostile, scary, or uncomfortable.

Harassment can include:

  • Offensive jokes about someone's race, religion, gender, etc.
  • Slurs or name-calling
  • Threats or intimidation
  • Unwanted physical contact
  • Displaying offensive images or objects
  • Repeated unwelcome comments about someone's body or appearance

Harassment becomes illegal when:

  • It's severe or happens repeatedly
  • It creates a hostile work environment
  • It affects someone's ability to do their job

Sexual Harassment

Sexual harassment is a specific type of harassment. It's unwanted sexual attention or behavior at work.

Sexual harassment includes:

  • Unwanted touching, hugging, or physical contact
  • Sexual jokes, comments, or gestures
  • Asking someone out repeatedly after they've said no
  • Sending sexual messages, emails, or pictures
  • Staring at someone's body in a way that makes them uncomfortable
  • Offering job benefits in exchange for sexual favors

💡 Both men and women can be victims of sexual harassment. And it can happen between people of any gender.

What to Do If It Happens to You

If you experience discrimination or harassment, you have options.

1
Say something (if you feel safe). Tell the person their behavior is unwelcome. "Please don't say things like that to me."
2
Document everything. Write down what happened, when, where, and who saw it. Save any texts or emails.
3
Report it to your manager or HR. Most companies have a process for reporting. You can also report to a different manager if your manager is the problem.
4
If nothing changes, report outside the company. You can file a complaint with the EEOC (Equal Employment Opportunity Commission) or your state's human rights agency.

⚠️ Retaliation is illegal. Your employer cannot punish you, fire you, or treat you badly for reporting discrimination or harassment.

What If You See It Happening to Someone Else?

You might witness discrimination or harassment happening to a coworker. You can help.

Ways to help as a bystander:

  • Interrupt the situation if it's safe — change the subject, ask the victim to help you with something
  • Check on the person later — "Hey, I saw what happened. Are you okay?"
  • Offer to be a witness — "I saw that too. I'll back you up if you report it."
  • Report it yourself — You can report what you witnessed to HR

🤝 Speaking up makes a difference. When people stay silent, harassers think their behavior is acceptable. When people speak up, it helps create a better workplace for everyone.

Where to Get Help

📞 Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC)

Federal agency that handles discrimination complaints

Website: eeoc.gov • Phone: 1-800-669-4000

📞 Missouri Commission on Human Rights

State agency for Missouri discrimination complaints

Website: labor.mo.gov/mohumanrights

📞 Your Company's HR Department

Usually your first step for reporting workplace issues

Time limits: You usually have 180–300 days to file a complaint with the EEOC after the incident. Don't wait too long.

Day 4 Review: Discrimination & Harassment

🤔 Think About It #1:

A coworker keeps making jokes about your religion even after you asked them to stop. Is this harassment?

Answer: Yes. Repeated offensive jokes about someone's religion that continue after you've asked them to stop is harassment.

🤔 Think About It #2:

You report harassment to HR, and the next week your hours get cut. Is this legal?

Answer: No. This sounds like retaliation, which is illegal. You can report this to the EEOC.

🤔 Think About It #3:

What should you do if you experience harassment at work?

Answer: Tell the person to stop (if safe), document everything, report to your manager or HR. If nothing changes, report to the EEOC.

Unions & Worker Protections

Organizations and laws that protect workers

What Is a Union?

A union (or labor union) is an organization of workers who join together to have a stronger voice at work.

What unions do:

  • Negotiate with employers for better pay, benefits, and working conditions
  • Protect workers from unfair treatment
  • Represent workers if there's a dispute with the employer
  • Advocate for workplace safety and fair policies

💡 Think of it this way: One worker asking for a raise might be ignored. But if ALL the workers ask together through a union, the employer has to listen.

How Unions Work

If your workplace has a union:

  • You may be asked to join (or pay fees even if you don't join)
  • The union negotiates a contract with your employer
  • The contract spells out pay, benefits, hours, and rules
  • If there's a problem, you can ask the union to help

Union dues:

Union members usually pay dues — a small amount from each paycheck that funds the union. This pays for negotiators, lawyers, and union staff.

🔹 Jobs that often have unions:

  • Teachers and school employees
  • Nurses and healthcare workers
  • Construction workers
  • Factory workers
  • Government employees
  • Grocery store workers

What Is OSHA?

OSHA stands for the Occupational Safety and Health Administration. It's a government agency that makes sure workplaces are safe.

🛡️
OSHA requires employers to:
  • Provide a workplace free from serious hazards
  • Follow all OSHA safety standards
  • Provide safety training in a language workers understand
  • Provide required safety equipment
  • Post OSHA information where workers can see it
  • Report serious injuries and deaths to OSHA

💡 If your workplace is unsafe, you can file a complaint with OSHA. You can do this anonymously, and your employer cannot punish you for it.

Website: osha.gov • Phone: 1-800-321-OSHA (6742)

Labor Laws That Protect You

There are many laws that protect workers. Here are some of the most important ones:

LawWhat It Does
Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA)Sets minimum wage, overtime pay, and child labor rules
Title VII of the Civil Rights ActProhibits discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, national origin
Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA)Protects people with disabilities from discrimination
Age Discrimination in Employment ActProtects workers 40 and older from age discrimination
Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA)Allows unpaid leave for family/medical reasons at larger employers

💡 You don't need to memorize these. Just know that the law is on your side if you're being treated unfairly.

Workers' Compensation

Workers' compensation (or "workers' comp") is insurance that pays for your medical bills and lost wages if you get hurt at work.

If you're injured at work, workers' comp can cover:

  • Doctor visits and hospital bills
  • Medicine and medical equipment
  • Physical therapy
  • Part of your wages while you can't work
  • Disability payments if you can't fully recover

Important:

  • Report your injury to your employer RIGHT AWAY
  • Fill out an incident report
  • Your employer must tell you how to file a workers' comp claim
  • You're covered even if the injury was your fault (in most cases)

Your Right to Organize

The law protects your right to join (or not join) a union and to advocate for better working conditions.

You have the right to:

  • Join a union
  • Talk to coworkers about working conditions, pay, or problems
  • Wear union buttons or t-shirts (in most cases)
  • Choose NOT to join a union

Your employer CANNOT:

  • Fire you for joining or supporting a union
  • Threaten you for talking about working conditions
  • Spy on union activities
  • Promise benefits to discourage union activity

⚠️ If you think your rights have been violated, you can file a complaint with the National Labor Relations Board (NLRB) at nlrb.gov.

Your Personal Information at Work

What employers need, what to protect, and how to stay safe

Information Your Employer Needs

When you start a job, you'll need to provide some personal information. This is normal and required by law.

Information you'll typically provide:

  • Full legal name
  • Address
  • Date of birth
  • Social Security Number (SSN)
  • Bank account info (for direct deposit)
  • Emergency contact
  • Proof of identity (ID, driver's license, passport)
  • Proof you can work in the U.S. (Form I-9)

💡 This information is needed to: Put you on the payroll, withhold the right amount of taxes, verify your identity, and pay you correctly.

Your Social Security Number (SSN)

Your Social Security Number is a 9-digit number that's unique to you. It's very important and very sensitive.

Your SSN is used for:

  • Taxes — employers report your wages to the IRS using your SSN
  • Social Security benefits — tracking your future retirement benefits
  • Background checks
  • Opening bank accounts
  • Getting credit cards or loans

🚨 PROTECT YOUR SSN!

  • Don't carry your Social Security card in your wallet
  • Don't give it out unless absolutely necessary
  • If someone steals your SSN, they can steal your identity

Direct Deposit

Direct deposit is when your paycheck goes straight into your bank account. Most employers offer (or require) this.

Benefits of direct deposit:

  • You don't have to go to the bank to deposit a check
  • Money is available on payday — no waiting for a check to clear
  • No risk of losing a paper check
  • More secure than carrying a paper check

To set up direct deposit, you need:

  • Your bank's routing number (identifies the bank)
  • Your account number (identifies your specific account)
  • These are on your checks or in your banking app

⚠️ Double-check your account numbers! If you enter the wrong number, your pay could go to the wrong account.

Tax Forms You'll Fill Out

When you start a job, you'll fill out forms that tell your employer how much tax to withhold from your paycheck.

📄 W-4 Form (Federal)

Tells your employer how much federal income tax to withhold.

You fill this out when you're hired. You can update it anytime.

📄 MO W-4 Form (Missouri)

Tells your employer how much state income tax to withhold.

📄 I-9 Form (Employment Eligibility)

Proves you're legally allowed to work in the United States.

You'll need to show ID documents (like a driver's license AND Social Security card, OR a passport).

💡 Ask for help if you need it. These forms can be confusing. Your employer or HR can answer questions.

Keeping Your Information Safe

Your personal information is valuable. Criminals try to steal it for identity theft — using your info to open accounts, steal money, or commit fraud.

✅ DO:

  • Only give SSN when required
  • Keep documents in a safe place
  • Shred papers with personal info before throwing away
  • Use strong passwords
  • Check your pay stubs for accuracy
  • Check your bank accounts regularly

❌ DON'T:

  • Carry your SSN card in your wallet
  • Give SSN over the phone unless YOU called
  • Click links in suspicious emails
  • Leave documents lying around
  • Share passwords with anyone
  • Give info to people who randomly call or email

What If Your Info Is Stolen?

If you think someone has stolen your personal information, act quickly.

1
Contact your bank if money or accounts are involved. They can freeze accounts and watch for fraud.
2
Place a fraud alert on your credit reports. Contact one of the three credit bureaus: Equifax, Experian, or TransUnion.
3
Report it to the FTC (Federal Trade Commission) at IdentityTheft.gov. They'll help you make a recovery plan.
4
File a police report if there's significant theft or fraud.

Signs your info may have been stolen:

  • Bills or statements for accounts you didn't open
  • Charges on your bank account you don't recognize
  • Calls from debt collectors about debts that aren't yours
  • Your tax return is rejected because one was already filed with your SSN
