Reading Work Schedules

Understanding when you work and how to manage your schedule

What Is a Work Schedule?

A work schedule is a chart or list that tells you when you're supposed to work. It shows your days, times, and sometimes your job assignments.

A schedule usually shows:

  • Which days you work
  • What time you start
  • What time you end
  • Sometimes: your position or station that day

💡 Your schedule is YOUR responsibility. Your boss won't call you every day to remind you. You need to know when you work and show up on time.

Where to find your schedule:

  • Posted on a wall or bulletin board at work
  • Sent by text, email, or an app (like When I Work, HotSchedules)
  • Given to you on paper

How to Read a Work Schedule

Here's an example of a typical weekly schedule:

BURGER PALACE — Week of Feb 3–9

Employee Mon Tue Wed Thu Fri Sat Sun
Jake B. OFF 4-9 4-9 OFF 5-10 11-5 OFF
Maria S. 9-3 9-3 OFF 9-3 OFF 10-4 10-4
Tyler R. 5-10 OFF 5-10 5-10 5-10 OFF OFF

🔹 Reading Jake's schedule:

  • Jake works 4 days this week
  • Tuesday: 4:00 PM to 9:00 PM
  • Wednesday: 4:00 PM to 9:00 PM
  • Friday: 5:00 PM to 10:00 PM
  • Saturday: 11:00 AM to 5:00 PM
  • He's OFF Monday, Thursday, and Sunday

Understanding Your Hours

Knowing how to count your hours helps you understand how much you'll get paid.

How to count hours:

If you work 4:00 PM to 9:00 PM, that's 5 hours.

If you work 11:00 AM to 5:00 PM, that's 6 hours.

🔹 Let's count Jake's hours this week:

  • Tuesday: 4-9 PM = 5 hours
  • Wednesday: 4-9 PM = 5 hours
  • Friday: 5-10 PM = 5 hours
  • Saturday: 11 AM-5 PM = 6 hours
  • Total: 21 hours this week

⚠️ Important: If you take a break, that time might be unpaid. For example, if you work 11-5 but take a 30-minute lunch, you might only get paid for 5.5 hours.

Checking Your Schedule Regularly

Schedules can change! You should check your schedule every week to know when you work.

Good habits for checking your schedule:

  • Check as soon as it's posted — usually by Thursday or Friday for the next week
  • Take a photo of the schedule with your phone
  • Write it down in a calendar or planner
  • Set alarms on your phone for each shift
  • Double-check before each shift in case it changed

🚨 "I didn't know I was scheduled" is NOT a good excuse. It's your job to know your schedule. If you miss a shift because you didn't check, that's on you.

Requesting Time Off

Need a day off for an appointment, family event, or vacation? You need to request it in advance.

1
Ask EARLY — at least 1-2 weeks ahead (more for vacations).
2
Fill out a request form if your job has one, OR talk to your manager directly.
3
Be polite and specific. "I need to request off Saturday, February 15th for a family event."
4
Wait for approval. Your request might be denied if too many people are off.
5
Check the schedule to confirm you got the day off. Don't assume!

⚠️ Requesting time off is NOT the same as telling them you won't be there. You're ASKING — they can say no. If it's denied, you still have to work.

Shift Trades & Shift Coverage

Sometimes you can't work a scheduled shift. One option is to find someone to cover for you.

How shift trades work:

  • You ask a coworker if they can work your shift
  • If they agree, you BOTH tell your manager
  • The manager must approve it
  • Only then is the trade official

✅ DO:

  • Ask your manager's permission first
  • Get the trade approved in writing or text
  • Return the favor when your coworker needs coverage

❌ DON'T:

  • Just not show up because someone "said" they'd cover
  • Trade shifts without telling your manager
  • Make up excuses to get out of shifts constantly

Until your manager approves it, the shift is still YOUR responsibility. If nobody covers it, YOU have to work it.

Workplace Policies

The rules every job has — and why they matter

What Are Workplace Policies?

Policies are the rules at your job. Every workplace has them. They tell you what you can and can't do.

Common workplace policies include:

  • Dress code — what to wear
  • Break policy — when and how long your breaks are
  • Phone/social media policy — rules about your phone at work
  • Attendance policy — rules about showing up

💡 Where to find workplace policies:

  • Employee handbook (booklet they give you when hired)
  • Signs posted at work
  • Training during orientation
  • Ask your manager if you're not sure

Following the rules is part of your job. Breaking policies can lead to warnings or getting fired.

Dress Code

A dress code tells you what to wear to work. Different jobs have different dress codes.

Type of JobTypical Dress Code
Fast food restaurantUniform shirt, black pants, non-slip shoes
Grocery storeCompany shirt or polo, khaki pants
Office jobBusiness casual — nice pants, collared shirt
WarehouseT-shirt, jeans, steel-toe boots

Common dress code rules:

  • No ripped jeans or shorts (unless allowed)
  • No flip-flops — closed-toe shoes required
  • Keep clothes clean and wrinkle-free
  • Name tag must be visible
  • Hair may need to be tied back (food service)

🚨 Showing up in the wrong clothes can mean being sent home. That means no pay for the day AND a mark against you.

Break Policies

Most jobs give you breaks during your shift. But there are rules about when, how long, and what you can do.

Typical break rules:

  • Short break (10-15 min): Usually paid. Stay on the property.
  • Meal break (30 min): Usually unpaid. You might be able to leave.
  • When you take breaks: Your boss decides, not you. Ask before going.

🔹 Example policy:

  • 4-hour shift = one 10-minute break
  • 6-hour shift = one 10-minute break + one 30-minute meal break
  • 8-hour shift = two 10-minute breaks + one 30-minute meal break

✅ DO:

  • Ask when you can take your break
  • Come back on time
  • Clock out for unpaid breaks

❌ DON'T:

  • Take longer breaks than allowed
  • Just disappear without telling anyone
  • Take "unofficial" breaks

Phone & Social Media Policies

Almost every job has rules about phone use. This is one of the most common things people get in trouble for.

Typical phone policies:

  • No personal phone use during your shift
  • Phones must be stored in locker, pocket, or break room
  • Only check your phone during breaks
  • No texting, scrolling, or watching videos while working

Social media rules:

  • Don't post negative things about your job or boss
  • Don't share customer information
  • Don't take photos or videos at work (especially of customers)
  • Don't post that you're "so bored at work" — your boss might see it

🔹 If you need your phone for an emergency:

Tell your boss ahead of time: "I'm expecting an important call from my doctor. If my phone rings, can I step away to answer it?"

Attendance Policies

We talked about attendance in Unit 2, but every job has specific policies written down.

Common attendance policies:

  • Call-in policy: How far in advance to call if you're sick (often 2 hours before your shift)
  • No-call no-show: Not showing up without calling — usually an automatic write-up or termination
  • Tardy policy: How many times you can be late before action is taken
  • Point system: Some jobs give you "points" for absences. Too many points = fired

🔹 Example point system:

  • Late (1-15 min): 0.5 points
  • Late (15+ min): 1 point
  • Absent with call: 1 point
  • No-call no-show: 3 points
  • 8 points = terminated

🚨 A "no-call no-show" is one of the worst things you can do. Some jobs will fire you for just ONE.

Other Common Policies

Here are some other rules you might encounter at work:

Smoking/Vaping:

  • Only in designated areas
  • Only during your break
  • Never in front of customers

Food & Drinks:

  • Usually only allowed in the break room
  • No eating while working
  • Water bottles might be allowed — ask

Dating Coworkers:

  • Some jobs have rules about this
  • Usually you can't date someone you supervise or who supervises you
  • Keep personal relationships out of work time

💡 When in doubt, ask your manager. It's better to ask than to assume and get in trouble.

Day 2 Review: Workplace Policies

🤔 Think About It #1:

You show up to work in shorts and flip-flops. The dress code requires pants and closed-toe shoes. What will probably happen?

Answer: You might be sent home to change — and you won't get paid for that time. You could also get a warning.

🤔 Think About It #2:

You're scheduled for a 6-hour shift. How many breaks do you probably get?

Answer: Usually one short break (10-15 min) and one meal break (30 min). But check your workplace's specific policy!

🤔 Think About It #3:

What is a "no-call no-show" and why is it so bad?

Answer: Not showing up to work AND not calling. It's one of the worst things you can do — some jobs fire you for just one.

Workplace Safety

Staying safe on the job and knowing what to do in emergencies

Why Workplace Safety Matters

Every year, millions of people get hurt at work. Many of these injuries could have been prevented.

Common workplace injuries:

  • Slips, trips, and falls (wet floors, cluttered walkways)
  • Cuts (from knives, box cutters, broken glass)
  • Burns (from hot equipment, grease, chemicals)
  • Lifting injuries (back injuries from lifting wrong)
  • Repetitive strain (doing the same motion over and over)

💡 Your employer is required to keep you safe. But you also have to follow the safety rules and watch out for yourself.

Breaking safety rules can:

  • Get you hurt
  • Get a coworker hurt
  • Get you fired

Reading Safety Signs

Workplaces use color-coded signs to warn you about dangers. Learn what they mean!

☠️ DANGER Serious harm or death
⚠️ WARNING Could cause injury
⚡ CAUTION Minor injury possible
🚪 SAFETY Emergency exits, first aid
ℹ️ NOTICE General information

🔹 Common signs you'll see:

  • "Wet Floor" — Caution, slippery
  • "Employees Only" — Restricted area
  • "Emergency Exit" — Way out in an emergency
  • "Fire Extinguisher" — Location of fire equipment
  • "No Smoking" — Smoking not allowed here

Reporting Hazards

A hazard is anything that could hurt someone. If you see a hazard, you need to do something about it.

Examples of hazards:

  • A spill on the floor
  • A broken piece of equipment
  • Boxes blocking an exit
  • A frayed electrical cord
  • Something stacked too high that could fall
1
If you can fix it safely, do it. Mop up a small spill. Move boxes out of the way.
2
If you can't fix it, warn people. Put up a sign, block the area, tell coworkers to stay away.
3
Tell your manager right away. "There's a big spill in aisle 3. I put up the wet floor sign but it needs to be mopped."

⭐ Reporting hazards shows you're responsible and looking out for others. Your boss will appreciate it.

Emergency Exits & Fire Safety

Every workplace must have emergency exits. You need to know where they are BEFORE an emergency happens.

On your first day at any job, find out:

  • Where are the emergency exits? (There's usually more than one)
  • Where is the fire extinguisher?
  • Where do employees meet outside if there's a fire?
  • What does the fire alarm sound like?

If you hear a fire alarm:

  • Stop what you're doing
  • Leave immediately — don't grab your stuff
  • Use the nearest exit — don't use elevators
  • Go to the meeting spot so they can count everyone
  • Don't go back inside until you're told it's safe

⚠️ Never block an emergency exit. Don't put boxes, equipment, or anything else in front of exit doors.

What to Do If You Get Hurt at Work

If you get injured at work — even a small injury — you need to report it.

1
Tell your manager right away. Even if it seems minor. "I cut my finger on a box. It's not bad, but I wanted to let you know."
2
Get first aid. Most workplaces have a first aid kit. Your manager can help you find it.
3
Fill out an incident report. This is a form that documents what happened. It protects you AND the company.
4
If it's serious, get medical help. Your employer might send you to a specific clinic. Ask what to do.

Why you MUST report injuries:

  • If the injury gets worse later, there's a record of what happened
  • You might be covered by workers' compensation (money for medical bills)
  • It helps the company fix the hazard so nobody else gets hurt

Basic Safety Tips

✅ Safe Habits:

  • Wear the right shoes (closed-toe, non-slip)
  • Lift with your legs, not your back
  • Clean up spills immediately
  • Keep walkways clear
  • Use equipment the right way
  • Ask for help with heavy items
  • Report broken equipment
  • Know where the first aid kit is

❌ Unsafe Habits:

  • Running at work
  • Ignoring wet floor signs
  • Using broken equipment
  • Lifting too much by yourself
  • Blocking exits with boxes
  • Wearing headphones so you can't hear warnings
  • Horsing around
  • Not reporting injuries

Day 3 Review: Workplace Safety

🤔 Think About It #1:

You see a spill in the break room. What should you do?

Answer: If you can clean it safely, do it. If not, put up a warning sign and tell your manager.

🤔 Think About It #2:

The fire alarm goes off. What do you do?

Answer: Stop working, leave immediately using the nearest exit (not elevator), go to the meeting spot, and don't go back until you're told it's safe.

🤔 Think About It #3:

You cut your hand at work. It's not serious, but it is bleeding. What should you do?

Answer: Tell your manager, get first aid, and fill out an incident report — even if it seems minor.

Clocking In & Out

How time clocks work and why accurate time matters

What Is Clocking In?

Clocking in is how you record when you start and end your work shift. This is how your employer knows how many hours to pay you.

EMPLOYEE TIME CLOCK
9:58 AM
ENTER ID OR SWIPE CARD

Types of time clocks:

  • Punch clock: You insert a paper time card
  • Digital keypad: You enter your employee number
  • Badge/card reader: You swipe or tap an ID card
  • Fingerprint scanner: You scan your finger
  • Computer/App: You log in on a computer or phone app

When to Clock In and Out

You should clock in when you're ready to start working — not when you walk in the door.

The right order:

1
Arrive at work (10-15 min early)
2
Put your stuff away, use the bathroom, get your uniform on
3
Clock in when you're ready to work (at or just before your start time)
4
START WORKING

At the end of your shift:

1
Finish your work / get approval to leave
2
Clock out
3
THEN get your stuff and leave

🚨 Don't clock in and THEN go put your stuff away, get coffee, or chat. Once you're clocked in, you should be working.

Don't Clock In Too Early or Too Late

Most jobs have rules about when you can clock in. Usually you can clock in a few minutes early, but not too much.

🔹 Example policy:

Your shift starts at 10:00 AM.

  • Okay: Clock in between 9:55 and 10:00
  • Not okay: Clock in at 9:45 (too early)
  • Not okay: Clock in at 10:10 (late)

Why can't you clock in too early?

  • The company has to pay you for that time
  • You might hit overtime (over 40 hours), which costs them more
  • It's considered dishonest if you do it on purpose

💡 If your manager asks you to come in early or stay late, that's different — they're approving the extra time. Always ask before working extra hours.

Clocking Out for Breaks

If you take an unpaid break (like a 30-minute meal break), you usually need to clock out.

How meal breaks work:

1
Ask your manager when to take your break
2
Clock OUT when your break starts
3
Take your break (30 minutes — time it!)
4
Clock back IN when your break ends
5
Get back to work

Short breaks (10-15 min) are usually paid. You typically do NOT clock out for these. But check your workplace's policy.

⚠️ If you forget to clock back in after break, tell your manager right away. They can fix it. Don't just ignore it — you might not get paid for those hours.

What Is "Time Theft"?

Time theft is stealing time from your employer — being paid for time you didn't actually work.

Examples of time theft:

  • Buddy punching: Having a coworker clock you in when you're not there
  • Clocking in early and then sitting around, not working
  • Taking longer breaks than allowed without clocking out
  • Not clocking out when you leave early
  • Personal tasks on the clock: Texting, shopping online, scrolling social media
  • Rounding up: Writing down that you worked 4:00-9:00 when you really worked 4:15-8:45

🚨 Time theft is taken very seriously. It's a form of stealing. You can be fired immediately for it — and in some cases, you could face legal trouble.

Common Clock-In Mistakes (And How to Avoid Them)

MistakeWhy It's a ProblemWhat to Do Instead
Forgetting to clock in You might not get paid Make it a habit — clock in first thing
Forgetting to clock out Shows extra hours you didn't work Tell your manager immediately so they can fix it
Clocking in too early Costs the company money / looks dishonest Wait until your start time (or 5 min before)
Having someone else clock you in It's time theft — you could be fired Always clock yourself in and out
Not clocking out for unpaid breaks You get paid for time you weren't working Clock out, set a timer, clock back in

💡 If you make an honest mistake, tell your manager right away. They can usually fix it. The problem is when people do it on purpose or hide it.

Day 4 Review: Clocking In & Out

🤔 Think About It #1:

Your shift starts at 2:00 PM. You arrive at 1:50 PM. When should you clock in?

Answer: Around 1:55-2:00 PM — after you've put your stuff away and you're ready to work. Not at 1:50 while you're still getting settled.

🤔 Think About It #2:

Your friend is running late and asks you to clock them in. Should you do it?

Answer: NO. This is "buddy punching" and it's time theft. You could both get fired.

🤔 Think About It #3:

You forgot to clock out for your 30-minute lunch break. What should you do?

Answer: Tell your manager right away so they can fix your time. Don't ignore it.
