Day 1 • ELA Concept

Language Power: Can Words Shape Thought?

Denotation vs. connotation

Denotation = dictionary meaning. Connotation = the feeling a word carries. “Cheap” and “affordable” denote the same thing — and feel completely different.

Euphemism & loaded language

A euphemism softens something harsh (“let go” for fired). Loaded language smuggles in judgment (“regime” vs. “government”). Both steer you before you notice.

The big question

If you have no word for an idea, is the idea harder to think? Powerful groups act like the answer is yes — that’s why controlling words is a way of controlling people.

Everyday version

“Pre-owned vehicle.” “Budget-friendly.” “Content.” Marketing renames things every day to change how you feel about them. Spot the rename, and you spot the strategy.

Today’s bridge: Ganku-eh refuses to keep someone she cannot question directly — in Winten-ah, language isn’t politeness, it’s survival and standing. Watch what changes for Alex the moment translation exists.

Day 2 • ELA Concept

Quote, Paraphrase, or Summarize?

MoveWhat it isWhen to use it
QuoteThe author’s exact words in “quotation marks”When the wording itself is powerful evidence
ParaphraseThe same idea, your words, about the same lengthWhen you need the idea but not the exact words
SummaryThe whole passage shrunk to its key points, your wordsWhen the reader needs the big picture fast

Example — one sentence, three ways

Original: “His first memory is an execution.”
Quote: Harden opens by revealing that Shin’s “first memory is an execution.”
Paraphrase: Harden opens by telling us the earliest thing Shin can remember is watching someone be killed.
Summary (of the whole preface): The preface introduces Shin’s childhood in Camp 14 through the executions he was forced to attend.

All three still need a citation — changing the words does not make an idea yours.

Today’s bridge: Dan translating for Alex’s life IS paraphrase with stakes: same meaning, new words, nothing added, nothing lost. Practice the skill the scene dramatizes — faithful restatement.

Day 3 • ELA Concept

Reading Visual Texts: Images Make Arguments

Images argue

Every designed image — a poster, an ad, a thumbnail, a meme — is trying to make you feel or believe something. It just argues faster than words.

The analysis checklist

  • Scale: what is biggest — and therefore “most powerful”?
  • Placement: what sits at eye level, front and center?
  • Color & light: what feels warm, cold, dangerous?
  • Caption: what do the words command you to feel?

Everyday version

A fast-food ad’s burger is shot from below, glistening, huge — scale and light making an argument no sentence makes. You are analyzed by images daily; today we analyze back.

Today’s bridge: Tonight’s ritual is a text with no words: torchlight, rhythm, synchronized dancers, food carried in procession. Read it with the visual checklist — scale, placement, light, movement — and ask what it argues.

Day 4 • ELA Concept

Anatomy of an Analytical Paragraph

PartJobSounds like
ClaimAn arguable idea — not a fact, not a summary“Alex’s preparation makes his risk feel safer than it is.”
EvidenceA quote or specific moment that supports the claim“For example, when he…” / “The text says…”
ExplanationConnect evidence to claim — the part that shows YOUR thinking“This shows…” / “This matters because…”

The most common mistake

Stopping after evidence. A quote never explains itself — the explanation is where the points live. Aim for at least as many explanation sentences as evidence sentences.

Transitions that glue it together

For example • This shows • In addition • However • As a result • Ultimately

Today’s bridge: Apply-level: you own the claim. “Belonging” is the key word — your evidence must show it growing, resisted, or both. Two chapters minimum.
