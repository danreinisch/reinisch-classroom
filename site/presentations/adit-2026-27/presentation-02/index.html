Day 1 • ELA Concept

Tone vs. Mood: The Author’s Voice, the Reader’s Feeling

Tone = the author’s attitude

Revealed by word choice. “The politician slithered to the podium” vs. “strode to the podium” — same event, opposite tone.

Mood = the reader’s feeling

The atmosphere the text creates in you: dread, hope, unease, excitement. Built from setting, pacing, and imagery.

The chain reaction

Author picks words (tone) → words build atmosphere → atmosphere creates feeling (mood). When a book makes you feel watched, that was engineered — find the words that did it.

Everyday version

“We need to talk.” Four neutral words — and instant dread. Tiny word choices carry huge mood. Authors weaponize this on every page.

Today’s bridge: This chapter’s mood swings hard: menace in the log, absurd comedy in Alex’s Kansas joke, dread at the hobbling. Track the words that move the temperature.

Day 2 • ELA Concept

Cause & Effect: Nonfiction’s Engine

The pattern

Nonfiction writers constantly argue because X, therefore Y. Your job: separate what happened (effect) from what produced it (cause) — and notice when one effect becomes the next cause.

Signal words

because • as a result • therefore • led to • consequently • so that • in order to • this meant that

Chains, not pairs

Real causes come in chains: scarce food → competition → broken trust → isolation. Trace the whole chain and you understand a system, not just an event.

Everyday version

Phone dies → alarm doesn’t ring → late to school → missed quiz. One cause, cascading effects. Authors build these chains on purpose — find the first domino.

Today’s bridge: Today, danger builds a chain: the karak-ta attack → the old man defends Alex → something between them changes. Trace how shared danger becomes the first cause of trust.

The Turn: When Captors Become Protectors

The moment

The karak-ta flock attacks. The older man — Alex’s guard — pushes Alex ahead to safety and turns to face the diving creature himself, dropping it with one perfectly timed swing.

Why it matters

A guard protects his prisoner at his own risk. That single action outweighs pages of suspicion — actions are the only language these two share.

The comic mirror — the giant cockroach

That night, Doken-ak saves Alex again (never pee on the trees in Kragdon-ah). The camp grins but doesn’t mock him openly. Note what laughter-without-cruelty tells us about these people — and how Alex’s inner monologue takes it.

Character development (RL.2.D): Chapter 5 ends with Alex’s thought: “Home — and Amy — felt very far away.” The adventure has weight now. Watch how the author balances wonder, fear, and homesickness.

Day 3 • ELA Concept

Structure: Parallel Plots, Foreshadowing & Dramatic Irony

ToolWhat it isWhat it does to the reader
Parallel plotsTwo storylines told side by sideYou keep reading to see them collide
ForeshadowingEarly hints of what’s comingPlants unease you can’t explain yet
Dramatic ironyThe reader knows something the characters don’tCreates suspense: “look behind you!”

Everyday version

In a horror movie, the audience sees the shadow behind the door — the character doesn’t. That gap between what you know and what they know is dramatic irony, and directors and novelists both farm it for tension.

Structure is a choice. Whenever a story is told out of order, or in two threads, ask: what does this arrangement do to me that straight order wouldn’t?

Day 4 • ELA Concept

Anatomy of an Analytical Paragraph

PartJobSounds like
ClaimAn arguable idea — not a fact, not a summary“Alex’s preparation makes his risk feel safer than it is.”
EvidenceA quote or specific moment that supports the claim“For example, when he…” / “The text says…”
ExplanationConnect evidence to claim — the part that shows YOUR thinking“This shows…” / “This matters because…”

The most common mistake

Stopping after evidence. A quote never explains itself — the explanation is where the points live. Aim for at least as many explanation sentences as evidence sentences.

Transitions that glue it together

For example • This shows • In addition • However • As a result • Ultimately

Choose Your Level

Foundation — 4–6 sentences

Use one detail and the frames: “At first Alex thinks ___.” “But when ___, he begins to ___.” “This shows ___.”

Supported — one developed paragraph

Use two details from different chapters — one about danger or captivity, one about care or trust — and explain how together they change Alex’s understanding.

Extension — multi-paragraph response

Use two or three details across chapters. Also consider: is Alex’s growing trust wisdom — or the only option a captive has? Where does the text leave it uncertain?
