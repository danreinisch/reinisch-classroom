Skill Reminder: Sentence Types

TypeWhat It IsExample
SimpleOne complete thought"Alex heard a voice."
CompoundTwo thoughts joined by FANBOYS (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so)"He heard a voice, and he jumped up."
ComplexOne main idea + one dependent idea joined by because, when, although, if, while"When he heard the voice, he jumped up."

Skill Reminder: Transition Words

  • Time order: first, next, then, finally, meanwhile, afterward
  • Cause/effect: because, so, therefore, as a result, consequently
  • Contrast: however, but, although, on the other hand, while
  • Addition: also, furthermore, in addition, moreover

Day 1: Sentences in Action

Finding Sentence Types in Chapter 32

Simple Sentence

"Alex slipped the thong of his stone hammer over one wrist."

One subject (Alex), one action — one complete thought.

Compound Sentence

"The captain glanced at him, and all the game-playing was gone."

Two complete thoughts joined by "and" (FANBOYS).

Complex Sentence

"While the others slept, he stayed awake shoveling."

Dependent idea (while the others slept) + main idea (he stayed awake).

Skill Reminder: How to Identify Sentence Type

1) Count the complete thoughts. 2) Look for FANBOYS (compound) or words like when, while, because, although, if (complex).

Day 1: Transitions in the Chapter

Transition Words in Chapter 32

  • "Finally the boat stopped its sharp turn" — shows time
  • "Instead, he said, 'Can they?'" — shows contrast
  • "He hurried back to the rear, so everyone could hear" — shows cause/effect

What Transitions Do

They connect ideas and show the relationship between them. Without transitions, writing feels choppy and disconnected. With them, ideas flow smoothly.

Day 1: Author's Craft

Why Does the Author Use Short Sentences During the Attack?

During calm moments: "Alex sat awake, swaying with the rhythm of the boat." — long, flowing.

During the attack: "He fell. He lost his footing." — short, punchy.

The author matches sentence length to the mood. Fast action = short sentences. Calm reflection = longer sentences. This is a tool YOU can use in your own writing.

Complex sentences use words like if, when, because, although, unless, while to connect a DEPENDENT idea to the MAIN idea. The dependent part cannot stand alone.

Day 2: Sentence Length and Mood

The Author Matches Sentence Length to Emotion

Despair: "He collapsed to his knees on the small patch of sand and dirt at the water's edge." — long, heavy, exhausted.

Horror: "One woman thrashed spectacularly, then her head zipped beneath the water, never to resurface." — one sentence = one death = maximum impact.

Dark humor: "'Or, we might just make a better buffet,' Versa-eh observed." — short and sharp.

Skill Reminder: Vary Your Sentence Length

Use SHORT sentences for dramatic moments. Use LONGER sentences for description and explanation. Mixing lengths keeps your writing interesting.

Day 2: Character Development

Even Alex Falls Into Despair

The author shows us that even the strongest leader can feel hopeless: "Alex, who seemed to have an endless amount of ideas and enthusiasm, had fallen into despair."

Why this matters: It makes Alex feel REAL. If he never struggled, he would not be a believable character.

Versa-eh's Dark Humor

"Or, we might just make a better buffet." Even in danger, Versa-eh uses humor. This tells us she handles fear by being clever and sharp.

Key Events

Day 3: Sentences in Action

Complex Sentence — Condition

"If I leave too early, I might not have drawn them far enough away."

The "if" creates a condition — what happens DEPENDS on timing.

Compound Sentence — Contrast

"He lost his nerve, turned, and ran."

Three quick actions chained together — matching the speed of the moment.

Skill Reminder: Sentence Types at a Glance

Simple: ONE complete thought. Compound: TWO thoughts + FANBOYS. Complex: Main thought + dependent thought (if/when/because/although).

Day 3: Transitions — Time Order

Alex's Escape Plan — Told with Transitions

First, Alex waded out into the water and began thrashing to attract the creatures.

Then, he watched the creatures swim toward him from across the island.

Finally, he turned and sprinted back toward the fire line, barely escaping.

Meanwhile, the others swam safely to the barge on the other side.

Time-order transitions help the reader follow a sequence of events. Without them, the order would be confusing.

Day 3: Monda-ak's Loyalty

"Monda-ak had refused to leave until Alex did."

Even with deadly creatures in the water, Monda-ak stays behind because his person has not left yet. The author uses this one simple sentence to show absolute loyalty — no long speech needed.

Skill Reminder: Sometimes the Most Powerful Writing Is the Shortest

One strong sentence can say more than a whole paragraph. When you want to make something hit hard, make it short.

Day 4: Writing

Your Written Response

Prompt

Paragraph Structure

  • Topic Sentence: State the main challenge or challenges.
  • Detail 1: Describe one challenge with a specific detail from the story.
  • Detail 2: Use a transition word to introduce a second challenge.
  • Detail 3: Add a third detail or explain how they overcame the challenges.
  • Conclusion: Wrap up your paragraph — what is the overall takeaway?

Day 4: Model Response

In Chapters 32-34, Alex and his group faced deadly challenges that tested their survival skills. First, pirates attacked their ship in the middle of the night, killed the captain, and forced all the passengers onto a sinking boat. Reference: Skill Definitions

Sentence Types

Simple sentence: One complete thought with a subject and a verb. Example: "Alex ran."

Compound sentence: Two complete thoughts joined by a FANBOYS word (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so). Always put a comma BEFORE the joining word. Example: "Alex ran, and Senta-eh followed."

Complex sentence: A main idea plus a dependent idea, connected by words like when, because, although, if, while, unless. If the dependent part comes first, put a comma after it. Example: "Because the ship was sinking, they had to swim."

Complete vs. Fragment

Complete sentence: Has a subject + verb + complete thought. "Alex swam to shore."

Fragment: Missing one of those. "Running to the beach." (no subject — WHO is running?)

Reference: Step-by-Step Strategies

How to Use Context Clues

1) Find the unknown word. 2) Read the sentence BEFORE and AFTER it. 3) Look for clues: examples, descriptions, or opposites. 4) Guess the meaning. 5) Re-read the sentence with your guess — does it make sense?

How to Identify Main Idea

1) Ask: What is MOST of this chapter about? 2) Look at how the chapter starts and ends. 3) Ignore small details — focus on the BIG picture. 4) Try to say it in ONE sentence.

How to Write a Strong Paragraph

1) Topic sentence — state your main idea. 2) Detail 1 — support with evidence. 3) Detail 2 — use a transition word. 4) Detail 3 — add more support. 5) Conclusion — wrap up without new info.

How to Check Your Grammar

1) Read each sentence out loud. 2) Check: subject + verb + complete thought? 3) Do subjects and verbs agree? (they WERE, not they WAS) 4) Capital at the start, period at the end? 5) Comma before FANBOYS in compound sentences?
