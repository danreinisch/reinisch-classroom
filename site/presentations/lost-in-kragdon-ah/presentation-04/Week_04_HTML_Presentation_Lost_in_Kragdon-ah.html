Day 1: First-Person Point of View

A New Grammar Concept

Understanding First-Person POV

WHAT IS FIRST-PERSON POINT OF VIEW? First-person point of view is when the story is told by a character IN the story, using words like "I," "me," "my," and "we." The narrator is a character who experiences the events.
WHY DO AUTHORS USE IT? First-person POV lets readers feel CLOSE to the main character. We experience events through their eyes. We can hear their thoughts and feelings directly.
HOW DO I RECOGNIZE IT? Look for the pronouns "I," "me," "my," and "we." If the narrator uses these words to describe themselves and their actions, it is first-person POV.

Day 1: Internal Thoughts

What Characters Think (Not Say)

Understanding Internal Thoughts

WHAT ARE INTERNAL THOUGHTS? Internal thoughts are what a character thinks but does NOT say out loud. In books, they are often shown in italics (slanted text) without quotation marks.
WHY SHOW INTERNAL THOUGHTS? Internal thoughts let readers know what characters are REALLY thinking - things they might not say to other characters. This helps us understand their true feelings.
HOW DO I SPOT THEM? Look for italicized text that shows thinking, not speaking. There are NO quotation marks because the character is not speaking out loud.

Day 1: Dialogue vs. Internal Thoughts

Spoken Words vs. Silent Thoughts

DIALOGUE (Spoken Out Loud)

"Where did you learn to speak this language?" Alex asked.

Has quotation marks. Other characters can hear it.

INTERNAL THOUGHT (Silent)

Smart kid. Maybe he doesn't remind me of myself after all.

No quotation marks. Only Alex (and the reader) knows this thought.

The Key Difference

Dialogue = Spoken out loud = Uses quotation marks " "

Internal Thoughts = Silent thinking = Uses italics, NO quotation marks

Day 2: Dialogue Punctuation Review

Quick Review of the Rules

The Four Rules of Dialogue

1
Quotation marks go around the EXACT words someone says.
2
Commas separate the dialogue from the tag (who said it).
3
Capitalize the first word inside quotation marks.
4
End punctuation (periods, question marks) goes INSIDE the closing quotation mark.

Day 2: Dialogue Examples from Chapter 12

Applying the Rules

"Whose little puppy is this?" Preta-eh asked.

Breaking It Down

  • " " = Quotation marks around spoken words
  • Whose = Capital letter starts the quote
  • ? = Question mark INSIDE the closing quote
  • Preta-eh asked = Dialogue tag (tells who spoke)

Quietly, Alex asked, "Why is that funny?"

Notice

When the tag comes FIRST, put a comma after the tag, then the opening quotation mark.

Day 2: More Complex Dialogue

Split Dialogue (Tag in the Middle)

"I should just kidnap you," she said, "and keep you here forever!"

Split Dialogue Explained

WHAT IS IT? When the dialogue tag interrupts (splits) the spoken words.
HOW TO PUNCTUATE?
  • First part ends with comma INSIDE quotes
  • Tag has commas on BOTH sides
  • Second part: lowercase if same sentence, capital if new sentence

Quick Check

In the example above, "and" is lowercase because it continues the same sentence.

If Preta-eh said two separate sentences, the second would start with a capital.

Can You Tell the Difference?

Which is Dialogue? Which is Internal Thought?

1. "Only if you're not downwind," Alex muttered.

Day 3: Action Beats

Actions Instead of "Said"

Understanding Action Beats

WHAT IS AN ACTION BEAT? An action beat is when a character DOES something instead of using a dialogue tag like "said" or "asked." The action tells us who is speaking.
WHY USE ACTION BEATS? They add movement and visual detail to dialogue. They show HOW a character feels without telling us directly.
HOW DO I PUNCTUATE THEM? Action beats are SEPARATE sentences. Use a period (not a comma) before or after the dialogue.

Day 3: Action Beat Examples

From Chapter 13

Preta-eh squealed. "Whose little puppy is this?"

Notice the Period

"Preta-eh squealed" is an action, not a dialogue tag. It gets a PERIOD, not a comma. Then the dialogue is a separate sentence.

Dialogue Tag (Comma)

"Whose little puppy is this?" Preta-eh asked.

"Asked" = dialogue tag = comma pattern

Action Beat (Period)

Preta-eh squealed. "Whose little puppy is this?"

"Squealed" = action = period before dialogue

Day 4: Writing Workshop

Putting It All Together

Writing Structure: Topic Sentence + Three Details + Conclusion

REMEMBER: Your writing should have FIVE parts:

  • Topic Sentence: A clear statement that answers the question.
  • Supporting Detail 1: First piece of proof from the story (use text evidence!).
  • Supporting Detail 2: Second piece of proof from the story.
  • Supporting Detail 3: Third piece of proof from the story.
  • Conclusion: Restate your main idea in different words.

Important Reminder

Your three supporting details should include text evidence - specific facts, events, or examples from the story that BACK UP your topic sentence!

Day 4: Writing Prompt 1

Your Prompt

How are Alex and Reggie similar? How are they different? Use details from Chapter 11 to compare the two time travelers.

Hints for Your Three Details

  • How did each one arrive in Kragdon-ah? (door/accident)
  • How long has each been in Kragdon-ah?
  • Why does Alex want to go home? Why doesn't Reggie?
  • What happened to each one right after arriving? (attacked by animals)

Topic Sentence Starter

"Alex and Reggie are similar in some ways but different in others."

Day 4: Writing Checklist

Check Your Work Before Submitting

Content Checklist

  • ☐ Do I have a clear topic sentence that answers the question?
  • ☐ Do I have three supporting details with text evidence?
  • ☐ Does each detail back up my topic sentence?
  • ☐ Do I have a conclusion that restates my main idea?

Grammar Checklist

  • ☐ Does every sentence start with a capital letter?
  • ☐ Does every sentence end with proper punctuation?
  • ☐ Are all character names capitalized?
  • ☐ If I used dialogue, did I use quotation marks correctly?
