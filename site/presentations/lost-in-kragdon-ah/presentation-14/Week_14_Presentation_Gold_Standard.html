Skills We Are Building

COMPARE = How things are alike (similar). CONTRAST = How things are different.

This week, characters change, roles reverse, and allies surprise us. We will track who people WERE versus who they BECOME.

A Note About This Week

These chapters contain the most emotional events in the book. We will discuss them respectfully and use them to understand how characters respond to loss.

You compare and contrast every day — deciding what to eat, which game to play, who to sit with. In reading, we use this skill to understand characters, events, and themes more deeply.

Day 1: Comparing Lanta-eh

What You'd EXPECT vs. What You SEE

What You Might EXPECT After a Year in Chains

Weak, confused, unable to think clearly. Scared, crying, needing help with everything. Angry at rescuers for taking so long.

What We ACTUALLY See

She smiles at the "ludicrousness" of Alex asking if SHE is okay. She knows about the secret passage. She gives orders with authority. She says "This was always going to be part of my life's journey."

CONTRAST: While most people would be broken after a year in chains, Lanta-eh emerges stronger, calmer, and more commanding than ever.

Skill Reminder: Contrast What You EXPECT with What HAPPENS

When a character defies your expectations, that is a powerful contrast. The bigger the gap between expectation and reality, the more it reveals about who they truly are.

Day 1: Comparing Allies

Versa-eh vs. The Ship Negotiator

The Negotiator (Chapter 30)

Tricked Alex, took his horses AND saddlebags, gave nothing extra. Helped himself.

Versa-eh and Harta-ak (Chapter 41)

Risked their own safety. Bought three horses. Waited at the gate not knowing if the group would even come that way. Helped selflessly.

When Alex asks "Why?" — Versa-eh says: "Because I want to." No deal, no payment, no obligation. Just choice.

Skill Reminder: Contrasting Two Characters Reveals Values

When you put two characters side by side who face a similar choice, their DIFFERENT actions reveal what they truly value. The negotiator valued profit. Versa-eh valued people.

Day 2: The Cruelest Irony

Comparing the End of Chapter 40 to Chapter 42

End of Chapter 40Chapter 42

Skill Reminder: Contrasting Expectations with Outcomes Creates Irony

When the person you LEAST expect to die is the one who does, that contrast creates emotional impact. The bigger the gap between expectation and reality, the harder it hits the reader.

Day 2: Werda-ak's Final Words

"Thank you for bringing me."

COMPARE what MOST people might say while dying:

  • "Why did you bring me here?"
  • "This is your fault."
  • "I wish I had stayed home."

CONTRAST with what Werda-ak ACTUALLY says:

  • Gratitude, not blame
  • Acceptance, not regret
  • Peace, not anger

His words reveal a character who valued the JOURNEY — the adventure, the friendship, the purpose — more than his own survival.

Day 2: Senta-eh Steps Up

How Alex and Senta-eh Handle Grief DIFFERENTLY

Alex's Response

Shuts down. Stops making decisions. Says "I don't care." Lies on blanket. Does not look for a weapon when he hears footsteps.

Senta-eh's Response

Pushes pain aside. Makes travel decisions. Leads them off the trail. Cleans wounds. Finds food and water. Takes over every leadership role.

CONTRAST: While Alex processes grief by shutting down, Senta-eh processes grief by taking action. Neither way is wrong — they are simply different ways people survive devastating loss.

Skill Reminder: Contrasting Reactions Reveals Character

When two characters face the SAME event but react DIFFERENTLY, those different reactions reveal who they fundamentally are. Same grief, different coping — and both are valid.

Day 2: Author's Craft — Short Chapters

The Power of Brevity

Chapter 42 is the SHORTEST chapter in the book — just a few hundred words covering Werda-ak's death.

COMPARE this to the long battle chapters (39-40) with pages of detailed action.

Why? The author matches length to emotional weight. The battle needed detail. The death needed silence. Sometimes the most powerful writing is the writing that stops.

Skill Reminder: You Can Compare the WRITING Itself

Compare and contrast is not just for characters — you can compare chapter lengths, sentence styles, and tone to understand the author's choices.

Day 2: "Unscathed" to "Gone"

CONTRAST: At the end of Chapter 40, the author specifically tells us Werda-ak is "unscathed" — the only healthy person in the group. Then in Chapter 42, he is the only one who dies.

Why the author does this: By establishing Werda-ak as the safest person, the author makes his death MORE shocking. The contrast between "completely fine" and "dead" is the widest possible gap — and that is what makes it devastating.

Day 3: The Healer vs. The Villagers

Two Sides of Human Nature

The Old Healer

Demands payment (fair). Provides excellent treatment. "He won't lose the arm." Professional exchange — fair value for money.

The Villagers

The healer tells them about the tiles. They grab farming tools. They charge at the group to rob and likely kill them. Greed turned into violence.

CONTRAST: The healer earned her payment through skill. The villagers tried to take theirs through force. Same village, same people, completely different choices.

Skill Reminder: Same Setting, Different Choices = Strong Contrast

When two groups in the SAME place make opposite decisions, it reveals that character is about CHOICE, not circumstance. The healer and villagers had the same opportunity — they chose differently.

Day 3: Alex's Two Fighting Styles

Alex vs. Draka-ak (Chapter 40)

Strategic. Patient. Faked a stumble to create an opening. Thought several moves ahead. Used intelligence.

Alex vs. The Villagers (Chapter 44)

Pure rage. Charges alone at an entire mob. Tears on his face. Kills without strategy. Looks around afterward "as if just coming awake." Used grief.

CONTRAST: While Alex fought Draka-ak with his MIND, he fights the villagers with his GRIEF. The violence is not about the villagers — it is about everything he has lost.

Skill Reminder: Comparing the SAME Character at Different Times

One of the most powerful comparisons is the same person in two different moments. The gap between "strategic Alex" and "grieving Alex" tells us more about the cost of loss than any description could.

Day 3: Author's Craft — Tears During Battle

The author describes Alex fighting with "tears of anger, frustration and mourning" on his face, then saying "Let's keep going" as if waking up.

Why include this detail? It shows readers that his violence comes from grief, not cruelty. He is a broken man lashing out, not a cold killer. The tears are what make the scene human instead of horrifying.

Day 4: Writing — Compare and Contrast

Prompt

Write about TWO characters from Chapters 41-44. Explain how they are SIMILAR or DIFFERENT using at least two specific details from the story. Use comparison words like "while," "however," "similarly," "on the other hand," or "in contrast."

Paragraph Structure

  • Sentence 1: Name both characters and state your main comparison point.
  • Sentence 2: Describe ONE character with a specific detail from the text.
  • Sentence 3: Use a comparison word to describe the OTHER character.
  • Sentence 4: Wrap up — what does this comparison reveal?

Day 4: Sentence Starters

Use These to Get Started

"While [character 1] _____, [character 2] _____."

"[Character 1] is similar to [character 2] because both _____."

"In contrast to [character 1], [character 2] _____."

"On the other hand, [character 2] _____."

"Unlike [character 1], [character 2] _____."

"Both [character 1] and [character 2] _____, but they differ in _____."

Day 4: Model Response

Alex and Senta-eh handle grief in very different ways after losing Werda-ak. While Alex shuts down emotionally and says "I don't care" when asked about what to do next, Senta-eh pushes her pain aside and takes over every leadership role — choosing where to camp, cleaning wounds, and keeping the group moving. In contrast to Alex, who cannot even reach for a weapon when he hears footsteps, Senta-eh stays alert and protective, showing that people process the same loss in completely different ways.

What Makes This Strong

  • Names BOTH characters being compared
  • Uses THREE comparison words (different, while, in contrast)
  • Gives SPECIFIC details for each person (not vague)
  • Wraps up with what the comparison reveals

Day 4: Writing Checklist

Compare/Contrast Writing Checklist

  • Did you name the TWO things you are comparing?
  • Did you explain how they are SIMILAR or DIFFERENT?
  • Did you include at least TWO specific details from the story?
  • Did you use at least ONE comparison word (while, however, but, in contrast)?

Grammar Checklist

  • Capital letter at the start of every sentence?
  • Punctuation at the end of every sentence?
  • Every sentence is COMPLETE? (Subject + Verb + Complete Thought)

Reference: Skill Definitions

Compare

What it means: Showing how two or more things are ALIKE or SIMILAR.

Signal words: similarly, likewise, both, also, in the same way, just as

Example: "Both Alex and Senta-eh grieve deeply for Werda-ak."

Contrast

What it means: Showing how two or more things are DIFFERENT.

Signal words: however, but, while, on the other hand, in contrast, unlike, whereas

Example: "While Alex shuts down, Senta-eh takes action."

Character Change Over Time

What it means: Comparing who a character WAS at one point to who they ARE at another point — tracking growth, decline, or transformation.

Example: Senta-eh went from being afraid of gentle horses to leading an army.

Reference: Step-by-Step Strategies

How to Compare Two Characters

1) Pick two characters. 2) Ask: What do they have in COMMON? 3) Ask: How are they DIFFERENT? 4) Find specific text details for each. 5) Use a comparison signal word to connect them. 6) Explain what the comparison REVEALS.

How to Track Character Change

1) Pick a character. 2) Describe who they were EARLY in the story. 3) Describe who they are NOW. 4) Identify the specific event that changed them. 5) Use "before" and "after" or "then" and "now" to frame the contrast.

How to Use a Comparison Table

1) Create two columns — one for each thing you are comparing. 2) List traits, actions, or events in rows. 3) Look for patterns: Where are they alike? Where do they differ? 4) Use the strongest contrasts as your writing evidence.

How to Write a Compare/Contrast Paragraph

1) Topic sentence — name both subjects and state your comparison. 2) Detail about Subject A. 3) Transition word + detail about Subject B. 4) Explain what the comparison reveals. 5) Wrap up without adding new information.
