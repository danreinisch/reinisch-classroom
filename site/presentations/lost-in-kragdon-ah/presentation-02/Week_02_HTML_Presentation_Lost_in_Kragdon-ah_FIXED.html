Day 1: Commas in Compound Sentences

Joining Two Complete Thoughts

Understanding Compound Sentences

WHAT IS IT? A compound sentence joins two complete thoughts (sentences that could stand alone) using a comma and a connecting word like "and," "but," or "so."
WHY DO WE USE IT? The comma tells the reader to pause slightly. It shows where one complete idea ends and another begins. Without it, readers might get confused about which words go together.
HOW DO I KNOW WHEN TO USE IT? Ask yourself: "Can both parts stand alone as their own sentences?" If YES, add a comma before the connecting word. If NO, do not add a comma.

Day 1: The Comma Test

Step-by-Step Instructions

The Two-Sentence Test

Before adding a comma, split the sentence at the connecting word. Can both parts be complete sentences on their own?

1
Find the connecting word (and, but, so, or, yet)
Example: "Alex ran to the river and he jumped in."
2
Split the sentence into two parts:
Part 1: "Alex ran to the river."
Part 2: "He jumped in."
3
Ask: Are both parts complete sentences?
"Alex ran to the river." ✓ Complete!
"He jumped in." ✓ Complete!
YES → Add a comma!

Correct Answer

✓ Alex ran to the river, and he jumped in.

Day 1: When NOT to Use This Comma

A Common Mistake to Avoid

Warning: Not Every "And" Needs a Comma!

Only use a comma when BOTH parts are complete thoughts. If the second part cannot stand alone, do NOT use a comma.

NO Comma Needed

✓ Alex ran to the river and jumped in.

Test it:

"Alex ran to the river." ✓ Complete

"Jumped in." ✗ NOT complete!

"Jumped in" has no subject - who jumped?

YES, Comma Needed

✓ Alex ran to the river, and he jumped in.

Test it:

"Alex ran to the river." ✓ Complete

"He jumped in." ✓ Complete

Both parts have a subject and action!

Quick Practice: Does This Need a Comma?

"Senta-eh grabbed Alex and pulled him to shore."

Answer: NO comma. "Pulled him to shore" is not a complete sentence by itself.

Day 2: Commas After Introductory Words

The Rule Explained

Understanding Introductory Words

WHAT IS IT? Introductory words are words or phrases that come at the very beginning of a sentence and set up what comes next. Common examples include: First, Second, Third, Then, Next, Finally, However, Therefore, After that, In the morning, Before long.
WHY DO WE USE A COMMA? The comma creates a small pause. It separates the "setup" part from the main sentence. This helps readers know where the introduction ends and the main idea begins.
HOW DO I USE IT? If a sentence starts with a transition word or time phrase, put a comma immediately after it, then continue with your main sentence.

Day 2: Introductory Words - Examples

Correct Usage

The Pattern

Introductory Word, + rest of the sentence.

Correct Examples from the Story

✓ First, Werda-ak climbed the tree.

✓ Then, he cut the rope.

✓ Next, he wrapped it around his body.

✓ Finally, he climbed back down.

✓ After that, Alex prepared his own jump.

Common Mistakes

✗ First Werda-ak climbed the tree.

Missing comma after "First"


✗ First, Werda-ak climbed the tree then, he cut the rope.

This should be two separate sentences, each with its own comma after the introductory word.

Day 2: Common Nouns vs. Proper Nouns

Understanding the Difference

Two Types of Naming Words

WHAT IS A COMMON NOUN? A common noun names a GENERAL person, place, thing, or idea. It does NOT name one specific thing. Examples: river, warrior, dog, boy, village, tree, bridge.
WHAT IS A PROPER NOUN? A proper noun names a SPECIFIC person, place, or thing. It is an actual name. Examples: Kranda-ah, Grunta-ak, Monda-ak, Werda-ak, Winten-ah.
WHY DOES THIS MATTER? Proper nouns ALWAYS start with a capital letter.
Common nouns do NOT need a capital letter (unless they start a sentence).

Day 2: Common vs. Proper Nouns - Examples

Side-by-Side Comparison

Common Noun (No Capital) Proper Noun (Capital Letter)
river Kranda-ah (name of this specific river)
warrior Grunta-ak (name of this specific person)
dog Monda-ak (name of this specific dog)
boy Werda-ak (name of this specific boy)
village, tribe Winten-ah, Rinta-ah (names of places)

Quick Test: Which Needs a Capital?

"The warrior crossed the kranda-ah river."

Answer: "warrior" = common noun (no capital). "kranda-ah" = proper noun (it's the river's name) → should be "Kranda-ah"

Day 2: The Apostrophe - Two Jobs

Understanding Apostrophes

Apostrophes Have Two Different Uses

JOB #1: SHOWING OWNERSHIP (Possessives) An apostrophe + s shows that something belongs to someone.
Example: Alex's plan = the plan that belongs to Alex
JOB #2: REPLACING MISSING LETTERS (Contractions) When we combine two words into one shorter word, the apostrophe shows where letters were removed.
Example: can't = can + not (the apostrophe replaces "no")
HOW DO I TELL WHICH IS WHICH? Ask: "Does something belong to someone?" → Possessive
Ask: "Are two words combined into one?" → Contraction

Day 2: Possessives - Showing Ownership

How to Form Possessives

The Rule for Singular Possessives

To show that something belongs to ONE person or thing, add 's (apostrophe + s) to the owner's name.

1
Identify the owner: Who does it belong to?
"The plan belongs to Alex."
2
Add 's to the owner's name:
Alex → Alex's
3
Put the owned thing after:
Alex's plan

Examples from the Story

Alex's plan = the plan that belongs to Alex

Werda-ak's knife = the knife that belongs to Werda-ak

Senta-eh's strength = the strength that belongs to Senta-eh

the village's bridge = the bridge that belongs to the village

Day 2: Contractions - Combining Words

How Contractions Work

The Rule for Contractions

When two words are combined into one shorter word, the apostrophe takes the place of the missing letters.

Two Words Contraction What's Missing?
can + not can't The letters "no" are replaced
do + not don't The letter "o" is replaced
we + will we'll The letters "wi" are replaced
I + am I'm The letter "a" is replaced
it + is it's The letter "i" is replaced

Tricky One: won't

will + not = won't (NOT "willn't")

This is an irregular contraction - the spelling changes completely!

Day 4: Writing Workshop

Applying Punctuation and Capitalization

Writing Structure: Topic Sentence + Three Details + Conclusion

  • Topic Sentence: A clear statement that answers the question.
  • Supporting Detail 1: First piece of proof from the story.
  • Supporting Detail 2: Second piece of proof from the story.
  • Supporting Detail 3: Third piece of proof from the story.
  • Conclusion: Restate your main idea in different words.

Punctuation and Capitalization Rules to Follow

  • Every sentence starts with a capital letter.
  • Every sentence ends with a . or ? or !
  • All names (Alex, Werda-ak, Kranda-ah) have capital letters.
  • Use apostrophes for ownership (Alex's) and contractions (can't).
  • Use commas after introductory words (First, Then,).
