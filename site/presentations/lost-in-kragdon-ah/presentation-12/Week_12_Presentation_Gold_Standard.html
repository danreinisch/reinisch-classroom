Skills We Are Building

This week we learn to make inferences — figuring out things the author does NOT say directly by combining clues from the text + what you already know = your conclusion.

The Inference Formula

The Skill We Are Building This Week

Skill Reminder: Three Steps to Making an Inference

1) Find a clue in the text — something the author says or describes. 2) Think about what you already know about the world. 3) Put the clue and your knowledge together to figure something out the author did not say directly.

An Inference Is NOT a Guess

If you cannot point to a specific clue from the text, it is a guess — not an inference. Good inferences are always supported by evidence.

Real-Life Inference

Clue: Your friend walks in late, soaking wet, no umbrella.

What you know: It was sunny when you arrived an hour ago.

Inference: It started raining, and your friend got caught in it.

Inference vs. Guess — Can You Tell?

A: "The captain's father was murdered because lifelong watermen do not drown, and the ruler had motive."

B: "The captain's father was killed by a sea monster."

A is an inference — it uses text clues + world knowledge. B is a guess — no mention of sea monsters anywhere.

Day 1: Author's Craft

What the Author Does NOT Say

The author never writes "The captain's father was murdered." Instead, he gives us CLUES and lets US figure it out. This is what makes inference such a powerful reading skill — the author trusts the reader to connect the dots.

When an author makes you WORK for the meaning, the conclusion hits harder than if they just told you.

Day 2: The Communication Network

Making Inferences About How It Works

Clues from the Text

  • Clue: Birds always fly back to where they were born
  • Clue: Breeding pairs are taken to new locations
  • Clue: Baby birds born there think THAT is home
  • Clue: Written messages are attached to their legs

Our Inference

This creates a two-way communication network. Birds in Location A fly to Location B, and birds born in B fly back to A. Alex compares it to a "telephone switchboard" — each city can send messages to many places.

Skill Reminder: Combining Multiple Clues

Sometimes one clue is not enough. The bird system requires combining FOUR separate clues to understand the full picture. Strong readers gather clues like a detective gathers evidence.

Day 4: Writing With Inferences

Prompt

Write about what you can figure out about a character from Chapters 35-37. Use at least two specific details from the text as evidence to support your conclusion.

Paragraph Structure

  • Sentence 1 (Conclusion): "Based on the story, I can figure out that [character] is [your conclusion]."
  • Sentence 2 (Clue #1): "One clue is when [specific detail from text]."
  • Sentence 3 (Clue #2): "Another detail that supports this is [specific detail]."
  • Sentence 4 (Wrap-up): "These clues show that [restate conclusion differently]."

Day 4: Model Response

Based on the story, I can figure out that the ruler is a dangerous and strategic leader who controls people through fear and information. One clue is that both the previous chief and the captain's father died under suspicious circumstances after opposing him, which suggests he eliminates anyone who challenges his power. Another detail is the bird communication network, which shows he spent years building a surveillance system across thousands of miles. These clues tell me he is not just strong — he is smart, patient, and willing to do anything to maintain control.

What Makes This Strong

  • States the conclusion clearly in the first sentence
  • Gives TWO specific text details as evidence
  • Explains WHY each clue supports the conclusion
  • Wraps up by restating the conclusion in different words

Day 4: Writing Checklist

Inference Writing Checklist

  • Did you state your CONCLUSION clearly?
  • Did you include at least TWO specific details from the chapters?
  • Did you explain HOW those details support your conclusion?
  • Did you use a phrase like "I can figure out that..." or "This tells me..."?

Grammar Checklist

  • Capital letter at the start of every sentence?
  • Punctuation at the end of every sentence?
  • Every sentence is COMPLETE? (Subject + Verb + Complete Thought)

Reference: Skill Definitions

Making an Inference

What it is: Figuring out something the author does NOT say directly by combining clues from the text with what you already know about the world.

Formula: Text Clues + What You Know = Your Inference

Key difference from a guess: An inference is ALWAYS supported by specific text evidence. A guess has no evidence.

Drawing Conclusions

What it is: Combining MULTIPLE clues (sometimes from different chapters) to reach a bigger understanding about a character, event, or theme.

How it differs from inference: A conclusion typically requires more evidence and covers a broader understanding. One clue = inference. Multiple clues across chapters = conclusion.

Context Clues

What it is: Using the words and sentences AROUND an unknown word to figure out its meaning.

Steps: 1) Find the unknown word. 2) Read the sentence before and after. 3) Look for examples, descriptions, or opposites. 4) Guess the meaning. 5) Re-read — does your guess make sense?

Reference: Step-by-Step Strategies

How to Make an Inference

1) Read the passage carefully. 2) Find a CLUE — something the author says or describes. 3) Think about what you already KNOW about the world. 4) Put the clue + knowledge together. 5) State your inference. 6) CHECK: Can you point to the specific clue? If not, it is a guess.

How to Draw a Conclusion About a Character

1) Collect clues about the character from MULTIPLE places. 2) Look at what they SAY, what they DO, and what others say ABOUT them. 3) Ask: What do all these clues have in common? 4) State your conclusion about who they are. 5) Support it with at least two specific details.

How to Use Context Clues

1) Find the unknown word. 2) Read the sentence BEFORE and AFTER it. 3) Look for clues: examples, descriptions, or opposites. 4) Guess the meaning. 5) Re-read with your guess — does it make sense?

How to Write a Strong Paragraph

1) Topic sentence — state your main idea or conclusion. 2) Detail 1 — support with a specific text clue. 3) Detail 2 — add a second clue with a transition word. 4) Explain — tell WHY these clues support your conclusion. 5) Wrap up — restate your main point without new info.
