Before We Begin

Winston’s World

Winston Smith

A thin, 39-year-old Party worker in London, in the superstate of Oceania. Outwardly obedient. Inwardly — something else.

Big Brother

The face of the Party, on posters everywhere: BIG BROTHER IS WATCHING YOU.

The telescreen

A screen in every room that broadcasts propaganda and watches you back. It can never be turned off.

The four Ministries

Truth (propaganda), Peace (war), Love (law and order), Plenty (rationing). Notice: each name means its opposite.

Day 1 • ELA Concept

Reading Visual Texts: Images Make Arguments

Images argue

Every designed image — a poster, an ad, a thumbnail, a meme — is trying to make you feel or believe something. It just argues faster than words.

The analysis checklist

  • Scale: what is biggest — and therefore “most powerful”?
  • Placement: what sits at eye level, front and center?
  • Color & light: what feels warm, cold, dangerous?
  • Caption: what do the words command you to feel?

Everyday version

A fast-food ad’s burger is shot from below, glistening, huge — scale and light making an argument no sentence makes. You are analyzed by images daily; today we analyze back.

Today’s bridge: Oceania’s most powerful weapon in Chapter I isn’t a gun — it’s a poster. Run the checklist on Big Brother’s face as you listen.

Two Skills at Once: Listening Notes + Propaganda Analysis

Listening note format

Three columns: time | detail I heard | what it suggests.

Example: “~2 min | poster’s eyes follow you | you should feel watched even when no one is there.”

Reading a propaganda poster (RL.1.C)

  • Scale: the face is “more than a metre wide” — power through size.
  • Eyes: designed to follow the viewer — surveillance you carry with you.
  • Caption: a direct threat disguised as a slogan.

When you hear the telescreen described, add one row to your notes — detail, then inference.

Day 2 • ELA Concept

Tone vs. Mood: The Author’s Voice, the Reader’s Feeling

Tone = the author’s attitude

Revealed by word choice. “The politician slithered to the podium” vs. “strode to the podium” — same event, opposite tone.

Mood = the reader’s feeling

The atmosphere the text creates in you: dread, hope, unease, excitement. Built from setting, pacing, and imagery.

The chain reaction

Author picks words (tone) → words build atmosphere → atmosphere creates feeling (mood). When a book makes you feel watched, that was engineered — find the words that did it.

Everyday version

“We need to talk.” Four neutral words — and instant dread. Tiny word choices carry huge mood. Authors weaponize this on every page.

Today’s bridge: The Two Minutes Hate is a mood machine. Listen for the words that build frenzy — and the tone of a man describing his own helplessness.

Day 3 • ELA Concept

Theme: The Idea Under the Story

Topic (one word)

Loss. Curiosity. Duty. Survival. A topic is just a subject — it makes no point.

Theme (a complete claim)

“Loss can push people to build new purpose.” A theme says something about the topic that the story proves.

How writers plant themes

Themes are almost never stated. Trace them through repeated images, choices characters make, and consequences of those choices. When a pattern repeats, the author is pointing at something.

The portability test

A real theme statement works outside the book too. “Alex misses Amy” is plot. “Keeping a promise can cost the people you love” is theme.

Day 4 • ELA Concept

Anatomy of an Analytical Paragraph

PartJobSounds like
ClaimAn arguable idea — not a fact, not a summary“Alex’s preparation makes his risk feel safer than it is.”
EvidenceA quote or specific moment that supports the claim“For example, when he…” / “The text says…”
ExplanationConnect evidence to claim — the part that shows YOUR thinking“This shows…” / “This matters because…”

The most common mistake

Stopping after evidence. A quote never explains itself — the explanation is where the points live. Aim for at least as many explanation sentences as evidence sentences.

Transitions that glue it together

For example • This shows • In addition • However • As a result • Ultimately

Choose Your Level

Supported — one well-developed paragraph

Use chunked directions, the guided organizer, vocabulary support, and the evidence bank. One clear claim + one strong piece of evidence + explanation.

Extension — 2–3 analytical paragraphs

Reduce scaffolds. Make a nuanced claim, use multiple pieces of evidence, and address uncertainty or complexity — for example, whether any private thought in Oceania can stay private.

Sentence starters: “Orwell makes private thought dangerous by ___.”  “For example, ___.”  “This shows ___.”
