Day 1 • ELA Concept

Characterization: How Authors Build People

Direct characterization

The narrator tells you flat out: “Ben was brave.” Rare, and less interesting.

Indirect characterization

The author shows you and lets you conclude. Remember STEAL: what a character Says, Thinks, their Effect on others, how they Act, how they Look.

Everyday version

A friend says “I’m not mad” — then answers every text with one word. Words say one thing; behavior says another. You believe the behavior. So do readers.

Motivation — the why

Behind every action is a want or a fear. Find what a character wants most and fears most, and their choices start making sense.

Today’s bridge: Parsons is a foil — a character built for contrast. Run STEAL on him today, then hold him against Winston and Syme: three men, three ways of surviving the same system.

Day 2 • ELA Concept

Cause & Effect: Nonfiction’s Engine

The pattern

Nonfiction writers constantly argue because X, therefore Y. Your job: separate what happened (effect) from what produced it (cause) — and notice when one effect becomes the next cause.

Signal words

because • as a result • therefore • led to • consequently • so that • in order to • this meant that

Chains, not pairs

Real causes come in chains: scarce food → competition → broken trust → isolation. Trace the whole chain and you understand a system, not just an event.

Everyday version

Phone dies → alarm doesn’t ring → late to school → missed quiz. One cause, cascading effects. Authors build these chains on purpose — find the first domino.

Today’s bridge: Build the Party’s own logic chain today: private loyalty → divided allegiance → a loyalty the Party cannot audit → forbidden. Follow the chain and the strangest rules become coldly rational.

Day 2 • Mini-Lesson

Why Regulate the Private?

What the chapter establishes

Winston’s diary turns to his marriage to Katharine — a union of duty, arranged around the Party’s rules, empty of affection. Marriage exists to serve the Party; personal attachment between spouses is treated as suspect. Winston’s memories of seeking any genuine human connection are written as confession — he cannot stop the pen.

The Party’s reasoning (RL.1.A — use its own words)

A private loyalty is a rival government of two. Strong personal bonds create an allegiance the Party cannot see into or redirect — so bonds themselves become the crime. Energy that might flow between people must flow to Big Brother instead.

Academic frame for this chapter: we analyze the system’s design — what the Party regulates and why — not the private details of Winston’s past. The diary’s function matters most: writing what cannot be said aloud, to no reader, at mortal risk.

Day 3 • ELA Concept

Central Idea: Nonfiction’s Main Point

Topic (one or two words)

School lunch. Prison camps. Social media. Just the subject — no point made.

Central idea (a complete point)

“The district’s new lunch program is failing students.” A claim the author develops with stacked evidence.

How authors build it: evidence stacking

Nonfiction writers pile up facts, examples, statistics, and testimony that all push the same direction. Your job: notice the direction the pile is pushing.

Everyday version

A friend lists: the coach benched them twice, cut their minutes, skipped their name at awards. They never say “the coach dislikes me” — but that’s the central idea of the evidence.

Today’s bridge: Winston’s diary entry today is an argument — a claim about the proles with evidence stacked under it. Treat it exactly like a nonfiction text: find his central idea, grade his receipts, and mark the gap he can’t close.

Day 4 • ELA Concept

Anatomy of an Analytical Paragraph

PartJobSounds like
ClaimAn arguable idea — not a fact, not a summary“Alex’s preparation makes his risk feel safer than it is.”
EvidenceA quote or specific moment that supports the claim“For example, when he…” / “The text says…”
ExplanationConnect evidence to claim — the part that shows YOUR thinking“This shows…” / “This matters because…”

The most common mistake

Stopping after evidence. A quote never explains itself — the explanation is where the points live. Aim for at least as many explanation sentences as evidence sentences.

Transitions that glue it together

For example • This shows • In addition • However • As a result • Ultimately

Today’s bridge: This EQ has two halves joined by one idea: uncontrolled loyalty. The Party fears it in Party members — and Winston finds it surviving among the proles. Build your claim on that hinge.

Week 3 Quick Reference

The story so far

The Party’s reach runs all the way into marriage and private feeling — loyalty itself is the threat. And in the one place the Party doesn’t bother to look, Winston finds loyalty still alive: the proles, 85 percent of everything, guarded only by the Party’s contempt. Hope exists. He just can’t find the door to it.
