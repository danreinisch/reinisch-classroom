Day 1 • ELA Concept

Map the Central Argument

Claim

Modern war is designed to continue, not to produce decisive victory.

Reasoning

War absorbs labor and goods that could raise living standards.

Political effect

Scarcity preserves dependence, inequality, and hierarchy.

Evidence offered

Superstate geography, disputed territories, labor, production, and recurring conflict.

Today’s bridge: Argument analysis asks whether each reason actually connects the evidence to the claim—not merely whether the conclusion sounds intelligent.

Day 2 • ELA Concept

Unpack the Slogan as Reasoning

Ordinary meaning

War and peace are opposites.

Goldstein’s redefinition

Continuous external conflict creates stable internal conditions.

Mechanisms

Fear, scarcity, secrecy, obedience, and controlled science.

Conclusion

Permanent war becomes politically equivalent to internal “peace.”

Today’s bridge: When an argument uses a paradox, identify which term has changed meaning. Otherwise, clever phrasing can hide a weak connection.

Day 3 • ELA Concept

Two Sources, Different Jobs

Goldstein’s book

A fictional embedded political argument explaining cyclical class power and permanent war.

Eisenhower address

A real 1961 presidential farewell warning about “unwarranted influence” in a military-industrial system.

Shared concern

Military structures can concentrate power and shape public priorities.

Critical difference

Eisenhower warns within a constitutional democracy; Goldstein claims a total system intentionally preserves hierarchy.

Today’s bridge: Corroboration can support a narrower concern without proving every part of a broader claim.

Wrap-Up

Week 9 Quick Reference

The story so far

Goldstein’s text builds a coherent model: war consumes surplus, scarcity preserves rank, and the class cycle repeats. Eisenhower’s archival warning shows that concern about concentrated military influence exists beyond the novel, while its different context prevents it from proving Goldstein’s total explanation.

Keep asking

  • Which premise would most weaken the argument if challenged?
  • Does a companion source corroborate the whole claim or only one part?
  • Have I named the source’s authority, purpose, usefulness, and limit?
