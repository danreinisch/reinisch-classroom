Day 1 • ELA Concept

Tone vs. Mood: The Author’s Voice, the Reader’s Feeling

Tone = the author’s attitude

Revealed by word choice. “The politician slithered to the podium” vs. “strode to the podium” — same event, opposite tone.

Mood = the reader’s feeling

The atmosphere the text creates in you: dread, hope, unease, excitement. Built from setting, pacing, and imagery.

The chain reaction

Author picks words (tone) → words build atmosphere → atmosphere creates feeling (mood). When a book makes you feel watched, that was engineered — find the words that did it.

Everyday version

“We need to talk.” Four neutral words — and instant dread. Tiny word choices carry huge mood. Authors weaponize this on every page.

Today’s bridge: Track two feelings today: the taut, patient mood of the preparation — and the surprising tone of reverence when the trap springs. Watch what the team feels about the animal they defeat.

Day 2 • ELA Concept

Characterization: How Authors Build People

Direct characterization

The narrator tells you flat out: “Ben was brave.” Rare, and less interesting.

Indirect characterization

The author shows you and lets you conclude. Remember STEAL: what a character Says, Thinks, their Effect on others, how they Act, how they Look.

Everyday version

A friend says “I’m not mad” — then answers every text with one word. Words say one thing; behavior says another. You believe the behavior. So do readers.

Motivation — the why

Behind every action is a want or a fear. Find what a character wants most and fears most, and their choices start making sense.

Today’s bridge: Spiral review with higher stakes: STEAL under fire. Today Alex’s Acts do all the talking — especially the act of taking the most dangerous role himself. Ask what that choice reveals… and what it costs.

Day 3 • ELA Concept

Theme: The Idea Under the Story

Topic (one word)

Loss. Curiosity. Duty. Survival. A topic is just a subject — it makes no point.

Theme (a complete claim)

“Loss can push people to build new purpose.” A theme says something about the topic that the story proves.

How writers plant themes

Themes are almost never stated. Trace them through repeated images, choices characters make, and consequences of those choices. When a pattern repeats, the author is pointing at something.

The portability test

A real theme statement works outside the book too. “Alex misses Amy” is plot. “Keeping a promise can cost the people you love” is theme.

Today’s bridge: Spiral review, final step: turn this week’s pattern into a theme statement. Topic: consequences. The chapters keep showing who volunteers to share them — build your claim there.

Day 4 • ELA Concept

Anatomy of an Analytical Paragraph

PartJobSounds like
ClaimAn arguable idea — not a fact, not a summary“Alex’s preparation makes his risk feel safer than it is.”
EvidenceA quote or specific moment that supports the claim“For example, when he…” / “The text says…”
ExplanationConnect evidence to claim — the part that shows YOUR thinking“This shows…” / “This matters because…”

The most common mistake

Stopping after evidence. A quote never explains itself — the explanation is where the points live. Aim for at least as many explanation sentences as evidence sentences.

Transitions that glue it together

For example • This shows • In addition • However • As a result • Ultimately

Today’s bridge: Apply-level: the prompt demands a decision. Pick ONE choice, argue it’s the most revealing, and trace its consequences — including the costs. Strong essays admit the limitation inside the strength.

Choose Your Level

Foundation — 4–6 sentences

One choice + frames: “Alex’s choice to ___ reveals ___.” “The consequence was ___.” “This shows his leadership is ___.”

Supported — one developed paragraph

One chosen choice, two details — the choice and its consequence — and an explanation that names both a strength and a limitation.

Extension — multi-paragraph response

Your chosen choice defended AGAINST one alternative (“a stronger candidate than ___ because…”), consequences traced across chapters, limitation addressed head-on.

Self-check: did I choose ONE choice and commit? • consequence traced with evidence • strength AND limitation named.
