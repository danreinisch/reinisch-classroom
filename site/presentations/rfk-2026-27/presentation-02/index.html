Day 1 • ELA Concept

Cause & Effect: Nonfiction’s Engine

The pattern

Nonfiction writers constantly argue because X, therefore Y. Your job: separate what happened (effect) from what produced it (cause) — and notice when one effect becomes the next cause.

Signal words

because • as a result • therefore • led to • consequently • so that • in order to • this meant that

Chains, not pairs

Real causes come in chains: scarce food → competition → broken trust → isolation. Trace the whole chain and you understand a system, not just an event.

Everyday version

Phone dies → alarm doesn’t ring → late to school → missed quiz. One cause, cascading effects. Authors build these chains on purpose — find the first domino.

Today’s bridge: Chapter 4 is a chain of decisions: can’t ferry the horses → someone must stay → who goes across? Each choice causes the next. Trace the chain and you trace Alex’s leadership.

Day 2 • ELA Concept

Structure: Parallel Plots, Foreshadowing & Dramatic Irony

ToolWhat it isWhat it does to the reader
Parallel plotsTwo storylines told side by sideYou keep reading to see them collide
ForeshadowingEarly hints of what’s comingPlants unease you can’t explain yet
Dramatic ironyThe reader knows something the characters don’tCreates suspense: “look behind you!”

Everyday version

In a horror movie, the audience sees the shadow behind the door — the character doesn’t. That gap between what you know and what they know is dramatic irony, and directors and novelists both farm it for tension.

Structure is a choice. Whenever a story is told out of order, or in two threads, ask: what does this arrangement do to me that straight order wouldn’t?

Today’s bridge: Ask the structure question today: why does the author spend a scene on the egg ceremony before the quest? Watch what the pause accomplishes — for Rinta-ah, and for our picture of Alex.

The Responsible Guide

What Alex does at the ceremony

He declines to take part — and instead guides the Rinta-ah through the egg experience, watching over each person, keeping everyone safe. After their months of grief, he gives them the experience and takes none of it.

What it reveals (RL.2.D)

Leadership as service: the guide stays clear-headed so others can rest. Compare Week 1’s Alex charging into the karak-ta — he is learning when a leader acts and when a leader watches.

Camp-craft as character

At the danta site: camp placed for shade and sightlines, guard rotations, Senta-eh hunting, no rushing. “We can stay here for as long as we need to and watch what’s going on.” The patience IS the plan.

Day 3 • ELA Concept

Quote, Paraphrase, or Summarize?

MoveWhat it isWhen to use it
QuoteThe author’s exact words in “quotation marks”When the wording itself is powerful evidence
ParaphraseThe same idea, your words, about the same lengthWhen you need the idea but not the exact words
SummaryThe whole passage shrunk to its key points, your wordsWhen the reader needs the big picture fast

Example — one sentence, three ways

Original: “His first memory is an execution.”
Quote: Harden opens by revealing that Shin’s “first memory is an execution.”
Paraphrase: Harden opens by telling us the earliest thing Shin can remember is watching someone be killed.
Summary (of the whole preface): The preface introduces Shin’s childhood in Camp 14 through the executions he was forced to attend.

All three still need a citation — changing the words does not make an idea yours.

Day 4 • ELA Concept

Anatomy of an Analytical Paragraph

PartJobSounds like
ClaimAn arguable idea — not a fact, not a summary“Alex’s preparation makes his risk feel safer than it is.”
EvidenceA quote or specific moment that supports the claim“For example, when he…” / “The text says…”
ExplanationConnect evidence to claim — the part that shows YOUR thinking“This shows…” / “This matters because…”

The most common mistake

Stopping after evidence. A quote never explains itself — the explanation is where the points live. Aim for at least as many explanation sentences as evidence sentences.

Transitions that glue it together

For example • This shows • In addition • However • As a result • Ultimately

Today’s bridge: Your claim should name at least two of the three tools — observation, preparation, collaboration — and your evidence should span more than one chapter.
