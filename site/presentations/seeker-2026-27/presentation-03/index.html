Day 1 • ELA Concept

Tone vs. Mood: The Author’s Voice, the Reader’s Feeling

Tone = the author’s attitude

Revealed by word choice. “The politician slithered to the podium” vs. “strode to the podium” — same event, opposite tone.

Mood = the reader’s feeling

The atmosphere the text creates in you: dread, hope, unease, excitement. Built from setting, pacing, and imagery.

The chain reaction

Author picks words (tone) → words build atmosphere → atmosphere creates feeling (mood). When a book makes you feel watched, that was engineered — find the words that did it.

Everyday version

“We need to talk.” Four neutral words — and instant dread. Tiny word choices carry huge mood. Authors weaponize this on every page.

Today’s bridge: Track the tonal turn today: the narration’s flat calm — then “He gasped!” One idea, and the mood of the entire chapter flips from cage to chess. Find the exact sentence where hope re-enters.

Day 2 • ELA Concept

Structure: Parallel Plots, Foreshadowing & Dramatic Irony

ToolWhat it isWhat it does to the reader
Parallel plotsTwo storylines told side by sideYou keep reading to see them collide
ForeshadowingEarly hints of what’s comingPlants unease you can’t explain yet
Dramatic ironyThe reader knows something the characters don’tCreates suspense: “look behind you!”

Everyday version

In a horror movie, the audience sees the shadow behind the door — the character doesn’t. That gap between what you know and what they know is dramatic irony, and directors and novelists both farm it for tension.

Structure is a choice. Whenever a story is told out of order, or in two threads, ask: what does this arrangement do to me that straight order wouldn’t?

Today’s bridge: Spiral review, biggest test yet: a three-year ellipsis PLUS a nonhuman narrator in one chapter. Ask of each move: what does this arrangement do to me that straight chronology and a human narrator couldn’t?

Day 3 • ELA Concept

Author’s Purpose & Credibility

Purpose: PIE

Authors write to Persuade, Inform, or Entertain — usually a mix. Ask: what does this author want to happen inside my head?

Credibility: can I trust this?

Check the receipts: Does the author name sources? Do other witnesses corroborate (confirm independently)? Is the author transparent about what they can’t verify?

Everyday version

Two YouTubers make the same claim. One shows documents and links sources; one says “trust me bro.” You already run credibility checks — we’re just making the checklist visible.

The counterintuitive rule

A source that admits the limits of its own story is usually more trustworthy, not less. Perfect stories should worry you.

Today’s bridge: Ask the purpose question of the whole design: why does Richards alternate a bound captive with a briefed president? What is each track built to make you FEEL — and what only exists when they collide?

Day 4 • ELA Concept

Anatomy of an Analytical Paragraph

PartJobSounds like
ClaimAn arguable idea — not a fact, not a summary“Alex’s preparation makes his risk feel safer than it is.”
EvidenceA quote or specific moment that supports the claim“For example, when he…” / “The text says…”
ExplanationConnect evidence to claim — the part that shows YOUR thinking“This shows…” / “This matters because…”

The most common mistake

Stopping after evidence. A quote never explains itself — the explanation is where the points live. Aim for at least as many explanation sentences as evidence sentences.

Transitions that glue it together

For example • This shows • In addition • However • As a result • Ultimately

Today’s bridge: Craft-analysis edition: your claim is about the AUTHOR’S choices (jump, POV, alternation), your evidence is scenes, your explanation names the effect on the reader. W.3.A.d bonus: vary your sentence lengths on purpose — a short one lands hard.
