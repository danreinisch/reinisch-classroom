Day 1 • ELA Concept

Structure: Parallel Plots, Foreshadowing & Dramatic Irony

ToolWhat it isWhat it does to the reader
Parallel plotsTwo storylines told side by sideYou keep reading to see them collide
ForeshadowingEarly hints of what’s comingPlants unease you can’t explain yet
Dramatic ironyThe reader knows something the characters don’tCreates suspense: “look behind you!”

Everyday version

In a horror movie, the audience sees the shadow behind the door — the character doesn’t. That gap between what you know and what they know is dramatic irony, and directors and novelists both farm it for tension.

Structure is a choice. Whenever a story is told out of order, or in two threads, ask: what does this arrangement do to me that straight order wouldn’t?

Today’s bridge: Richards gives you two clocks on page one: three years, and fifteen minutes. Feel what that arrangement does to your pulse — that’s structure working.

Juxtaposition: Why Put These Two Openings Side by Side?

Prologue: the probeChapter 1: the attack
ThreatUnknown, alien, patientHuman, familiar, immediate
TimelineThree years awayTen to fifteen minutes away
ToneCold, curious — almost playful: “It could hardly wait.”Tense, zealous, countdown
What we don’t knowWhat the probe wantsWhether anyone survives

Craft move: the reader now knows something no character knows — while humans attack each other, something bigger is coming. That gap is called dramatic irony, and Richards will make it pay off.

Day 2 • ELA Concept

Characterization: How Authors Build People

Direct characterization

The narrator tells you flat out: “Ben was brave.” Rare, and less interesting.

Indirect characterization

The author shows you and lets you conclude. Remember STEAL: what a character Says, Thinks, their Effect on others, how they Act, how they Look.

Everyday version

A friend says “I’m not mad” — then answers every text with one word. Words say one thing; behavior says another. You believe the behavior. So do readers.

Motivation — the why

Behind every action is a want or a fear. Find what a character wants most and fears most, and their choices start making sense.

Today’s bridge: Run STEAL on Kagan today — a man whose own Thinks-window calls him boring. Collect the evidence; the plot is about to audit it.

Day 3 • ELA Concept

Theme: The Idea Under the Story

Topic (one word)

Loss. Curiosity. Duty. Survival. A topic is just a subject — it makes no point.

Theme (a complete claim)

“Loss can push people to build new purpose.” A theme says something about the topic that the story proves.

How writers plant themes

Themes are almost never stated. Trace them through repeated images, choices characters make, and consequences of those choices. When a pattern repeats, the author is pointing at something.

The portability test

A real theme statement works outside the book too. “Alex misses Amy” is plot. “Keeping a promise can cost the people you love” is theme.

Revealed or Transformed? Reading a Character Under Pressure

The case for revealed

Chapter 2 showed a careful planner who puts Cynthia first. Under fire he plans again — the stove, the accelerants, the smoke — and puts a child first. Same man, higher stakes.

The case for transformed

Kagan expected to be a coward: he can “barely bring himself to jump from the high-dive.” Instead: “The world hadn’t slowed down. His mind had sped up.” He becomes someone he did not know existed.

Synthesis frame (we build it together)

Richards opens with three threats/choices — the probe, the attack, and Kagan’s dive — to establish that ___ and that individual sacrifice ___.

Day 4 • ELA Concept

Anatomy of an Analytical Paragraph

PartJobSounds like
ClaimAn arguable idea — not a fact, not a summary“Alex’s preparation makes his risk feel safer than it is.”
EvidenceA quote or specific moment that supports the claim“For example, when he…” / “The text says…”
ExplanationConnect evidence to claim — the part that shows YOUR thinking“This shows…” / “This matters because…”

The most common mistake

Stopping after evidence. A quote never explains itself — the explanation is where the points live. Aim for at least as many explanation sentences as evidence sentences.

Transitions that glue it together

For example • This shows • In addition • However • As a result • Ultimately
