Day 1 • ELA Concept

Explicit vs. Implicit: How Readers Know Things

Explicit — the text says it

Information stated directly. “Maria slammed the door.” We know the door slammed — it’s on the page.

Implicit — the text suggests it

An inference = detail from the text + what you already know. Slammed door + your life experience → Maria is probably angry. The text never said “angry.”

Everyday inference

You come home: wet umbrella by the door, muddy shoes on the mat. Nobody told you it rained — you inferred it. You do this skill all day already.

The one rule

Every inference must trace back to a detail you can point to. Evidence is your receipt — no receipt, no claim.

Today’s bridge: Nonfiction runs on the same engine: the Preface gives you explicit horrors, and asks you to infer what a system like this does to a child’s mind.

Day 1 • Mini-Lesson

Topic vs. Central Idea

Topic (one or two words)

Camp 14. Prison camps. Shin’s childhood.

A topic is just the subject — it makes no point.

Central idea (a complete point)

Camp 14 uses fear, rules, and hunger to control every part of a prisoner’s life — even family.

A central idea is a claim the author develops with evidence.

Evidence Harden stacksHow it builds the central idea
Everyone must attend executions — even children.Fear is public, planned, and mandatory.
The Ten Rules; rule one: escape = death.Rules replace law, family, and conscience.
Guards select who marries whom as a “reward.”Even love and family exist only by permission.

Day 2 • ELA Concept

Author’s Purpose & Credibility

Purpose: PIE

Authors write to Persuade, Inform, or Entertain — usually a mix. Ask: what does this author want to happen inside my head?

Credibility: can I trust this?

Check the receipts: Does the author name sources? Do other witnesses corroborate (confirm independently)? Is the author transparent about what they can’t verify?

Everyday version

Two YouTubers make the same claim. One shows documents and links sources; one says “trust me bro.” You already run credibility checks — we’re just making the checklist visible.

The counterintuitive rule

A source that admits the limits of its own story is usually more trustworthy, not less. Perfect stories should worry you.

Today’s bridge: Harden is a journalist. Today, watch his PIE purpose (inform — and quietly persuade the world to look) and grade his receipts.

Day 3 • ELA Concept

Quote, Paraphrase, or Summarize?

MoveWhat it isWhen to use it
QuoteThe author’s exact words in “quotation marks”When the wording itself is powerful evidence
ParaphraseThe same idea, your words, about the same lengthWhen you need the idea but not the exact words
SummaryThe whole passage shrunk to its key points, your wordsWhen the reader needs the big picture fast

Example — one sentence, three ways

Original: “His first memory is an execution.”
Quote: Harden opens by revealing that Shin’s “first memory is an execution.”
Paraphrase: Harden opens by telling us the earliest thing Shin can remember is watching someone be killed.
Summary (of the whole preface): The preface introduces Shin’s childhood in Camp 14 through the executions he was forced to attend.

All three still need a citation — changing the words does not make an idea yours.

Day 3 • Mini-Lesson

Objective vs. Subjective: Build a Clean Summary

Subjective (opinion added) — not a summary

“The horrible guards cruelly forced poor Shin to watch executions, which was absolutely evil.”

Objective (facts only) — the goal

“Harden describes Shin’s childhood in Camp 14, where attendance at executions was mandatory and food was scarce.”

Four-sentence summary formula

1. Author + text + topic  2. The central idea  3. Key detail one  4. Key detail two. No opinions, no “I think,” no judgment words.

Day 4 • ELA Concept

Anatomy of an Analytical Paragraph

PartJobSounds like
ClaimAn arguable idea — not a fact, not a summary“Alex’s preparation makes his risk feel safer than it is.”
EvidenceA quote or specific moment that supports the claim“For example, when he…” / “The text says…”
ExplanationConnect evidence to claim — the part that shows YOUR thinking“This shows…” / “This matters because…”

The most common mistake

Stopping after evidence. A quote never explains itself — the explanation is where the points live. Aim for at least as many explanation sentences as evidence sentences.

Transitions that glue it together

For example • This shows • In addition • However • As a result • Ultimately

Choose Your Level

Language Arts 2 SC — one developed paragraph

Use chunked directions, the organizer, and the evidence bank. State a clear claim, include at least one strong piece of evidence, and explain how it supports your claim.

Challenge / Extension — 2–3 analytical paragraphs

Develop a nuanced claim with multiple pieces of evidence. Address credibility or complexity — for example, how Harden handles the changes in Shin’s testimony.

Sentence starters: “Harden shows that Camp 14 reshapes ___ when ___.”  “This evidence suggests ___.” Self-check: claim → evidence → explanation → objective tone.
