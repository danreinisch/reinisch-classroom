Day 1 • ELA Concept

Structure: Parallel Plots, Foreshadowing & Dramatic Irony

ToolWhat it isWhat it does to the reader
Parallel plotsTwo storylines told side by sideYou keep reading to see them collide
ForeshadowingEarly hints of what’s comingPlants unease you can’t explain yet
Dramatic ironyThe reader knows something the characters don’tCreates suspense: “look behind you!”

Everyday version

In a horror movie, the audience sees the shadow behind the door — the character doesn’t. That gap between what you know and what they know is dramatic irony, and directors and novelists both farm it for tension.

Structure is a choice. Whenever a story is told out of order, or in two threads, ask: what does this arrangement do to me that straight order wouldn’t?

Today’s bridge: “This story begins a day earlier” — Harden uses a flashback. We already know where Shin ends up; now we watch him walk toward it. Ask what that dread-in-advance does to you as a reader.

Day 2 • ELA Concept

Tone vs. Mood: The Author’s Voice, the Reader’s Feeling

Tone = the author’s attitude

Revealed by word choice. “The politician slithered to the podium” vs. “strode to the podium” — same event, opposite tone.

Mood = the reader’s feeling

The atmosphere the text creates in you: dread, hope, unease, excitement. Built from setting, pacing, and imagery.

The chain reaction

Author picks words (tone) → words build atmosphere → atmosphere creates feeling (mood). When a book makes you feel watched, that was engineered — find the words that did it.

Everyday version

“We need to talk.” Four neutral words — and instant dread. Tiny word choices carry huge mood. Authors weaponize this on every page.

Today’s bridge: Notice Harden’s tone on the hardest pages: flat, precise, unadorned. He refuses to decorate suffering. Ask why understatement lands harder than outrage would — and what it leaves the reader to do.

Reading the Interrogation Analytically

What the chapter shows — in brief

The interrogators assume guilt by blood and demand a confession Shin cannot give. He is shown the family rap sheet — his uncles’ 1951 flight south is the “crime” his whole family has been paying for. What follows is torture; we will not rehearse its details. Shin, near death, finally says the one true thing: “I was the one who reported this. I did a good job.”

What it reveals (RI.2.C)

Ideas interact with events: inherited guilt (Week 2) meets power without accountability. The system never needed Shin to be guilty — it needed him to be guilty by definition. Truth only surfaces when a classmate’s corroboration — not Shin’s word — confirms it.

Track the one guard sentence Harden includes — “That little runt is really tough” — and weigh what even that flicker of admiration does and does not change.

Day 3 • ELA Concept

Characterization: How Authors Build People

Direct characterization

The narrator tells you flat out: “Ben was brave.” Rare, and less interesting.

Indirect characterization

The author shows you and lets you conclude. Remember STEAL: what a character Says, Thinks, their Effect on others, how they Act, how they Look.

Everyday version

A friend says “I’m not mad” — then answers every text with one word. Words say one thing; behavior says another. You believe the behavior. So do readers.

Motivation — the why

Behind every action is a want or a fear. Find what a character wants most and fears most, and their choices start making sense.

Today’s bridge: Uncle is a real man characterized almost entirely through Acts: the wooden spoon, the shared food, the massages, the whispered stories. Run STEAL on a real person today — nonfiction characterizes too.

Day 4 • ELA Concept

Anatomy of an Analytical Paragraph

PartJobSounds like
ClaimAn arguable idea — not a fact, not a summary“Alex’s preparation makes his risk feel safer than it is.”
EvidenceA quote or specific moment that supports the claim“For example, when he…” / “The text says…”
ExplanationConnect evidence to claim — the part that shows YOUR thinking“This shows…” / “This matters because…”

The most common mistake

Stopping after evidence. A quote never explains itself — the explanation is where the points live. Aim for at least as many explanation sentences as evidence sentences.

Transitions that glue it together

For example • This shows • In addition • However • As a result • Ultimately
